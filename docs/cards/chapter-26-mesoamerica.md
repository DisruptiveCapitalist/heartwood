# Chapter 26 — Across Cultures cards: Maya and Mesoamerican navigation

Handbook copy, written to Design's request of 5 November 2026. No course lesson
carries a Mesoamerican card, so the chapter had nothing labelled to build from.
Four cards follow, with the Olmec material as a separate note rather than a
fifth card, because the evidence will not carry a card — the note says why.

Format is the one the lesson exports use. **Do not restate any claim in any of
these cards without its label, its uncertainty and its sources.** Each card also
answers the two Part VI questions — what stays still, and what happens when it
is unavailable — because for this chapter the answers are unusually sharp.

One general warning before the cards. Mesoamerica attracts more confident
nonsense than any other region in Part VI, and the quickest way to catch it is
to ask what was measured and with what. Three of the four cards below name the
instrument and the sample size for that reason.

### Across cultures — THE MAYA LOWLANDS: BUILDINGS THAT POINT AT THE HORIZON
**Evidence label: WELL DOCUMENTED**

The label is on the pattern in the survey data. It is not on any individual
building's story, and the difference between those two things is the whole
argument of this card. What has been measured, systematically and recently: 248
orientations at 84 lowland sites in the first published tranche, and more than
300 at 106 sites by 2016. The method matters as much as the total. Rather than
quoting one azimuth per building, the surveyors measured several construction
lines on each structure, then converted azimuths to **declinations** using the
measured altitude of the real horizon and an allowance for refraction — which is
the step that makes two buildings on different ground comparable, and the step
that most single-building alignment claims skip.

The result is that the orientations are not scattered: they cluster, and they
cluster on the Sun's rising and setting positions on dates separated by
calendrically meaningful intervals. Two findings in that data should change how
the chapter is written. First, **the equinox is essentially absent**.
Declinations near 0° barely occur; what occurs is a group near ±1°, which
corresponds to dates two days after the March equinox and two days before the
September one — the "quarter days" that, with the solstices, cut the year into
four equal parts. Second, there are clusters near ±28°, beyond the Sun's
extreme of ±23.5°, which can only refer to Venus at its evening extremes (a
maximum declination of about ±27.5°, reached in an eight-year rhythm) or to the
Moon at its standstills (±28.5°). The well-known Venus claims about particular
buildings — the Caracol's windows at Chichén Itzá, the Governor's Palace at
Uxmal — belong here, as individual instances of a family that the survey data
support as a family.

For the course, this is Lesson 19's star compass built in stone: a direction
named by where something comes up, and then made permanent. The limit of the
method is equally instructive. For eighty years the "E-Group" assemblages were
read as solstice and equinox markers; the review that actually tested them found
the alignments were rarely accurate enough to do that job, and argued that
precise solar alignment was at most a minor and early part of what the
assemblages were for. A direction you cannot lose is not the same thing as a
measurement that is precise, and this architecture is the first kind.

**What stays still:** the horizon, and the buildings aimed at it. **When it is
unavailable:** nothing in the record says — and that is the point. These
sightlines are fixed to one spot. Carried anywhere else they are worthless,
which is the difference between an orientation calendar and an instrument.

> **What remains uncertain:** the individual stories, which is where popular
> accounts spend all their time. The equinox shadow on El Castillo at Chichén
> Itzá is a real and striking effect, but it is visible over several weeks
> either side of the equinoxes and is not evidence of an equinoctial alignment —
> and the systematic data say equinoctial alignments are the one thing Maya
> architecture avoids. The specific window azimuths claimed for the Caracol
> should be taken from the 1975 survey's own tables and read beside the 2013
> reassessment of Chichén Itzá; I could not verify those individual figures from
> a primary source for this card, and the chapter should not print them from a
> secondary one. More generally, whether any one building was oriented
> deliberately is not decidable from that building: the argument is statistical,
> it depends on the sample and on which lines were measured, and a chapter that
> quotes a single alignment should quote the method and the sample size with it.
> Which dates and which intervals the orientation calendar encodes is argued
> among the specialists who agree on everything else here.

> **Sources:** Šprajc and Sánchez Nava, "Orientaciones astronómicas en la
> arquitectura maya de las tierras bajas: nuevos datos e interpretaciones", in
> the XXV Simposio de Investigaciones Arqueológicas en Guatemala (2012), and
> their monograph of the same name, Instituto Nacional de Antropología e
> Historia (2015); the same authors, "Astronomy and Architecture in the Maya
> Lowlands", *Journal of Skyscape Archaeology* 2 (2016): 189–215; Aveni, Gibbs
> and Hartung, "The Caracol Tower at Chichén Itzá: An Ancient Astronomical
> Observatory?", *Science* 188 (1975): 977–985; Aveni, Dowd and Vining, "Maya
> Calendar Reform? Evidence from Orientations of Specialized Architectural
> Assemblages", *Latin American Antiquity* 14 (2003): 159–178; Aimers and Rice,
> "Astronomy, Ritual, and the Interpretation of Maya 'E-Group' Architectural
> Assemblages", *Ancient Mesoamerica* 17 (2006): 79–96; Aveni, *Skywatchers*
> (revised edition, 2001).

### Across cultures — THE MAYA CALENDAR, AND WHY IT IS NOT A NAVIGATOR'S CLOCK
**Evidence label: WELL DOCUMENTED**

The machinery first, because the numbers are exact and a reader can check every
one. A 260-day count (tzolkʼin) and a 365-day count (haabʼ) run together and
return to the same pairing every **18,980 days** — 52 haabʼ, 73 tzolkʼin, about
51.97 tropical years. Beside them runs the Long Count, a plain tally of days in
units of 1, 20, 360, 7,200 and 144,000 (that last about 394 years), which does
not repeat, so any date expressed in it is unambiguous for millennia. The Venus
table in the Dresden Codex is built on the same kind of meshing: a canonical
Venus period of **584 days**, five of which make 2,920 days — exactly eight
haabʼ — and the table runs 65 periods, **37,960 days**, which is 104 haabʼ and
exactly two calendar rounds.

Now the part that matters to a navigation handbook. A true synodic period of
Venus is **583.92 days**, so the canonical 584 runs fast by 0.08 of a day every
time round: 0.4 days in eight years, **5.2 days over the table's full 104-year
span — about one day every twenty years**. The table does not pretend otherwise:
it carries instructions to subtract days at stated points. That is a correction
applied to a count, on the authority of the sky.

Which is exactly the course's distinction, and it is worth being careful about
which course idea this attaches to. The tempting link is to Chapter 16 and
Lesson 26 — longitude is a clock problem, and here is a culture with a superb
clock. **That link is not honest and the chapter should not make it.** A
calendar counts days; longitude needs the time of day at a distant meridian. At
the latitude of Chichén Itzá, one hour of clock error is 842 nautical miles of
longitude — 969 statute — and a day of error is the entire globe. The honest
link is to **Lesson 28**: a quantity you carry accumulates error, a quantity you
measure does not, and a carried quantity therefore has to be reset against
something that stays put. The Dresden Venus table is a dead reckoning of a
planet with a correction routine built into it, which is the same relationship a
navigator has with a log line and a noon sight.

**What stays still:** Venus, the Sun, and the day-count itself, which is the
rarest kind of fixed reference — one made by people and kept by people. **When
it is unavailable:** cloud costs an observation but not the count. The count
runs on and drifts, at a day per twenty years, until the sky can be read again
and the correction applied. That is precisely the failure mode of dead
reckoning, and it is why the table has corrections at all.

> **What remains uncertain:** the exact rule of those corrections is debated —
> published readings differ over whether the subtractions are of 4 and 8 days or
> of other amounts, and at which stations. The table's anchoring to our own
> calendar rests on the Goodman–Martínez–Thompson correlation (commonly the
> constant 584,283, with 584,285 also defended); it is well supported but it is
> a correlation, not an observation, and it has been challenged in print.
> Whether 584 was adopted as an astronomical measurement or because it is the
> number that makes the calendar mesh is an open question, and the mesh is
> suspiciously neat. Finally, accuracy claims of the "two hours in four
> centuries" kind circulate widely with no method attached; they should not be
> repeated in the handbook, which can print the 0.08-day drift and the
> correction instead and be both more impressive and true.

> **Sources:** Bricker and Bricker, *Astronomy in the Maya Codices*, American
> Philosophical Society (2011); Thompson, *A Commentary on the Dresden Codex*
> (1972); Aveni, *Skywatchers* (revised edition, 2001); the Dresden Codex itself
> is digitised and freely readable at the Sächsische Landesbibliothek – Staats-
> und Universitätsbibliothek Dresden (facsimile, accessed 2026).

### Across cultures — SACBE 1, COBÁ TO YAXUNÁ, SEVENTH CENTURY
**Evidence label: DOCUMENTED REGIONALLY**

Labelled for a road and a region rather than a culture, deliberately: the
nearby Cobá-to-Ixil causeway averages 6.2 m wide against this one's 9.8, and
"Maya roads" is not a thing this card can describe. What it can describe is the
best-measured ancient road in the Americas. Lidar flown in 2017 and 2022, with
excavation and ground survey behind it: Sacbe 1 is **99,072 m** long, averages
**9.84 m** wide (8.8 to 10.7, standard deviation 0.39 m) and 0.82 m high, and
contains about **790,000 m³** of stone — roughly the volume of Cobá's five
largest monumental complexes put together. Five gaps totalling 942 m are later
stone robbing, not design.

And the headline a navigation handbook should take: **it is not straight, and we
now know by how much.** A straight line between the two termini would be
**98,060 m** on a bearing of **93°28′**. The road is 1,012 m longer than that —
about one per cent. There are **39 places where the direction changes by more
than 3°**, the largest change **17.5°**, the average **7.8°**, and the route
wanders far enough to leave a one-kilometre-wide survey corridor in three
places. Some of the turns are explicable: at Kauan, 31.2 km from the Cobá end, a
two-kilometre series of curves swings the bearing from about 280° to 259° to
pick up the next two sites; 4.9 km from Yaxuná it turns 11.85° right and then
10.2° left to gather a cluster of small settlements. Others are not: on one 7.3
km stretch with no settlements to link and no sinkholes to avoid, it changes
direction three times.

Put this card beside the Chacoan Great North Road in Lesson 28 and the chapter
has its best page. Those two roads are the same kind of object and the opposite
kind of evidence. Chaco's interest is a line held to within a few degrees over
fifty kilometres; here is a hundred-kilometre road whose builders plainly could
hold a line — several stretches run perfectly straight — and who declined to, at
a cost of one per cent in length, in order to touch the places that mattered. A
road is not a bearing. If the chapter prints one Mesoamerican number, print
99,072 against 98,060.

**What stays still:** the road. A causeway is the strongest answer to that
question in the whole book — a direction built into the ground, which needs no
sky and no instrument and cannot be lost in cloud. **When it is unavailable:**
you are in closed forest with no horizon and no long views, and because the road
bends, a bearing remembered from the last straight stretch will not take you to
either end of it. The road is not a shortcut through the problem of
wayfinding; it is the answer, and off it there is no answer at all.

> **What remains uncertain:** the date. No chronometric date comes from the
> causeway itself; the sixth-to-eighth-century range rests on settlement
> ceramics and epigraphy at the two ends, and the attribution to Cobá's
> seventh-century queen is the authors' argument, well made, rather than a dated
> fact. Why it bends is interpretation too, and the paper offers several
> candidates at once: linking existing settlements, reaching water, engineering
> around hummocks, and uneven execution by different work crews. The width,
> height and volume figures are averages taken from lidar elevation models
> rather than from excavation. Note also that the earlier ground survey — Villa
> Rojas and twelve men from Chan Kom, twenty days with a Brunton compass and a
> tape in 1933 — reported the road as nearly straight. It was careful work with
> the instruments available, and it was wrong in exactly the way a chapter about
> instruments should enjoy.

> **Sources:** Ardren, Hutson, Stanton, Rohrer and Lowry, "Sacbe 1 and Classic
> Maya Urban Culture", *Latin American Antiquity* 37 (2026): 22–40, open access;
> Villa Rojas, "The Yaxuná–Cobá Causeway", Carnegie Institution of Washington
> (1934); Hutson and Welch on the intersite causeways at Ucí (2021).

### Across cultures — THE YUCATÁN COAST AND THE BAY ISLANDS, 1502 AND BEFORE
**Evidence label: DOCUMENTED REGIONALLY**

There is one eyewitness description of a Mesoamerican trading vessel, and the
chapter should present it as exactly that. On 30 July 1502, off the Bay Islands
of Honduras, Columbus's fourth voyage met a canoe; the account comes from his
son Ferdinand's life of his father, published in 1571. As described: a dugout
from a single trunk, as long as a galley and about eight feet in the beam, with
a palm-leaf shelter amidships, some two dozen paddlers, women and children
aboard, and a cargo of cacao, copper bells and axes, cotton cloth and
obsidian-edged weapons. It is the first European mention of cacao. The Spanish
seized the canoe and took its elderly captain along as a pilot and interpreter —
which is itself the period's most reliable navigational instrument, and worth
saying out loud in a chapter about navigation.

What the archaeology adds is the route's framework rather than its practice:
ports and salt works around the peninsula — Isla Cerritos as Chichén Itzá's
north-coast outlet, Xcambó, Cozumel — and the distribution of obsidian, copper
and salt that implies circum-peninsular coastal trade. What it does not add is a
technique. No sailing directions survive, no instrument has been identified, and
there is no record of a method for crossing open water out of sight of land. The
honest sentence is that offshore navigation here is **undocumented**, which is
not the same claim as that it did not happen.

The course idea this belongs to is the one that needs no instruments at all.
Coasting is navigation by continuous contact, and the Yucatán's low shore sets
its scale: from a canoe, coastal woodland ten metres high meets the horizon at
about nine nautical miles and a bare limestone shore at about seven, so the
whole trade route is a corridor some fifteen miles wide in which the land is
rarely out of sight for long. Lesson 26's problem — a position that has to be
carried because nothing can be measured — does not arise on a coasting passage,
because there is no interval during which contact is lost.

**What stays still:** the coast, continuously. **When it is unavailable** —
night, a squall, an offshore set — the record describes nothing to fall back on,
which is consistent with what coasting traders everywhere do: hug the shore,
stop at dark, and wait out weather on a beach.

> **What remains uncertain:** the eyewitness is a son's book published
> sixty-nine years after the event, surviving through an Italian translation of
> a lost Spanish original, so the dimensions and the crew count are Ferdinand's
> figures and not measurements; sources even differ on the date of the
> encounter. That the canoe was Yucatecan is an inference from its cargo. The
> circum-peninsular trade circuit is a reconstruction from endpoints and goods,
> and the "maritime Maya" literature runs from careful port archaeology to
> reconstructions that travel well beyond their evidence — the chapter should
> keep those apart and name which it is citing. No pre-contact Maya seagoing
> canoe has been excavated, so the vessel itself is known only from this
> description, from depictions, and from small dugouts preserved in cenotes.

> **Sources:** Ferdinand Columbus, *Historie* (1571), in Keen's translation,
> *The Life of the Admiral Christopher Columbus by His Son Ferdinand* (1959);
> Andrews, Gallareta Negrón, Robles Castellanos, Cobos Palma and Cervera Rivero,
> "Isla Cerritos: An Itzá Trading Port on the North Coast of Yucatán, Mexico",
> *National Geographic Research* 4 (1988): 196–207; Sabloff and Rathje (eds), *A
> Study of Changing Pre-Columbian Commercial Systems: The 1972–1973 Seasons at
> Cozumel, Mexico* (1975); McKillop, *Salt: White Gold of the Ancient Maya*
> (2002).

## The Olmec lodestone: a note, not a card

The spec asks for Olmec claims to be treated separately and only where the
evidence supports them. It does not support a card, and here is the note it does
support.

**Evidence label: PLAUSIBLE BUT UNCERTAIN — and the navigational use is not
established.**

The object is M-160, a bar of polished hematite from San Lorenzo Tenochtitlán,
Early Formative, broadly 1400–1000 BC, grooved along its length and broken at
one end. Carlson's analysis in *Science* in 1975 showed that it does work as a
magnetic pointer, and argued that it looks utilitarian rather than decorative
and that no one who has examined it has proposed another function. He was also
explicit that establishing Olmec priority would depend on finding supporting
artefacts and on archaeomagnetic data for the period — neither of which has
arrived in the fifty years since.

So the honest sentence for print is that a single Early Formative artefact can
function as a magnetic pointer, that its use is unknown, and that nothing
connects it to finding one's way. One object from one site, with no context of
use, no second example in half a century, no depiction of such a thing in use,
and no reference to direction-finding by magnet anywhere in later Mesoamerican
sources. The circumstantial support offered for it is the family of Olmec site
alignments about 8° west of north — but a systematic orientation that could have
been set by the sky is not evidence of a compass, and the lowland survey in the
first card above is a demonstration of how readily sky references account for
such families. **And even granting the strongest version of the claim, orienting
a building is not navigating.** Keep the artefact; drop the compass.

Claims of Olmec transoceanic voyaging have no archaeological support and should
not appear, not even as a refutation at length — a sentence is the correct
amount of room to give them.

## Where the evidence is too thin for a card, and should be a sentence

Design asked to be told this rather than handed padding. Three topics:

**A Mesoamerican open-sea technique.** Nothing is documented: no star-course
lore recorded for navigation, no sailing directions, no instrument. The codices'
astronomy is calendrical and divinatory in its recorded uses, and reading it as
navigational is an inference nothing supports.

**Maya charts or route maps.** None survive, and the surviving maps in the
colonial record are land maps of a different kind entirely.

**"The Maya as sailors."** The vessel in the 1502 account is a paddled dugout
with a shelter, not a sailing craft, and the sail's arrival on this coast is
itself argued over. A chapter that wants a maritime Mesoamerica will have to
build it from ports, cargo and one canoe, and should say so.

## Notes for whoever draws the figures

**The sacbe figure should show both lines** — the road as surveyed and the
straight line between its ends — because the one per cent difference is the
point and is invisible unless drawn. Exaggerate the lateral scale and label the
exaggeration.

**The alignment figure should be a histogram of declinations, not a plan of one
building with an arrow on it.** The arrow is the genre's besetting sin: it shows
a reader a conclusion and hides the sample it came from. A histogram with the
Sun's ±23.5° marked, and the clusters at ±1° and ±28° visible, makes the
argument honestly and is no harder to draw.

**For the Venus table, draw the drift**: a straight count climbing away from the
true period at 0.08 days a cycle, with the corrections as the steps back down.
It is the same shape as the red line in Lesson 28, and the chapter is allowed to
say so.
