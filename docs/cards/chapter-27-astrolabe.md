# Chapter 27 — Across Cultures card: the astrolabe

Handbook copy, written to Design's request of 5 November 2026. Chapter 27's
other material is carried by Lessons 8 and 18 — Ibn Mājid and the kamal — and
the astrolabe is in neither, so the chapter's astrolabe section had nothing
labelled behind it.

Format is the one the lesson exports use. **Do not restate any claim in it
without its label, its uncertainty and its sources.**

One framing note before the card, because it is the whole reason the card is
worth writing. **The astrolabe that is famous and the astrolabe that went to sea
are two different objects**, separated by six centuries and by almost every
feature either of them has. Nearly every popular account of Arab navigation
merges them, and a handbook that merges them will have a navigator carrying an
instrument that cannot be used on a moving deck and does not measure the thing
he needs.

### Across cultures — THE ISLAMIC WORLD AND THE EASTERN ATLANTIC, 900 TO 1600
**Evidence label: WELL DOCUMENTED**

The **planispheric astrolabe** is an analogue computer for the sky. A flat
projection of the celestial sphere turns over an engraved plate cut for one
particular latitude, and with it an astronomer can read off the time from the
altitude of a star, find when a star or the Sun will rise or set, work out the
direction of Mecca, fix the hours of prayer, measure the height of a tower, or
cast a horoscope. The Islamic world developed it to its highest point from the
ninth century onward: the earliest dated Islamic example to survive is signed by
Nastulus and dated 315 AH, 927 or 928 CE. Note what that list of uses has in
common. Every one of them is performed standing still, and most of them need a
plate cut for the latitude you are already at. The instrument answers "what time
is it and which way is Mecca", not "where am I".

What the Indian Ocean's pilots actually carried was far simpler. Altitude was
measured in finger-widths — the *isbaʿ*, 1°36′ — on a **kamal**, often called
simply *khashaba*, "the wood": a small board on a knotted string, the string
held in the teeth so the board's distance from the eye is fixed, the lower edge
on the horizon and the upper on the star. Ibn Mājid's manual of 1490 is built on
altitudes of that kind, on a thirty-two point star rose for bearings, and on the
monsoon calendar; he recommends the sine quadrant as well. The kamal beats the
astrolabe at sea for a reason the course states in Lesson 14: a hanging
instrument on a heaving deck measures the deck. The kamal has nothing to hang.
It is referenced to the horizon and to the observer's own teeth, and both of
those move with the ship.

The instrument that did go to sea is the **mariner's astrolabe**, and it is
Iberian, late fifteenth century, and barely an astrolabe at all: a heavy brass
ring graduated in degrees with a sighting bar pivoted at the centre, hung from a
thumb ring so that gravity holds it vertical, its frame cut away so that wind
blows through instead of pushing it. It was adapted from the planispheric
astrolabe — the earliest examples still carry the shadow scales of the parent
instrument, with nothing left to use them for — but every computing part is
gone. It measures one angle. The oldest known example comes from the Sodré
wreck, a ship of the 1502 Portuguese squadron lost off Al Hallaniyah in Oman in
1503; it dates to between 1496 and 1501, and laser scanning found its scale cut
as **eighteen marks at five-degree intervals**. Five degrees of latitude is
**300 nautical miles**. Even reading confidently to a quarter of a division
leaves 75. Set that beside the kamal's finger at 96, and the conclusion is the
one that matters for the chapter: **around 1500, at sea, everything landed
within a degree or two — sixty to a hundred and twenty miles — whatever the
instrument's theory allowed.** The limit was the platform, not the brass.

**What stays still:** the altitude of a known body at its meridian passage, and
behind it gravity, which is what the thumb ring is really using. **When it is
unavailable** — cloud, or a sea too lively for a hanging instrument to settle —
there is nothing but the reckoning, which is Lesson 28's climbing line. Note
also what this instrument can never do: it cannot measure the angle *between*
two bodies, so no lunar distance, so no longitude, ever, by any refinement.

**On exchange:** this card is a two-way street and should be written as one. The
Portuguese instrument is a simplification of an instrument perfected in the
Islamic world, and the latitude method it serves — altitude of the Sun at noon,
corrected by a table of declinations — rests on the same astronomical tradition
that produced the *zīj* tables. Going the other way, when Portuguese ships
entered the Indian Ocean in 1498 they entered a sea whose practical navigation
literature was already written, and they used its pilots. Neither side invented
latitude sailing; what each had was a different answer to the question of what
you can read on a wet deck.

> **What remains uncertain:** whether planispheric astrolabes were used at sea
> to any real extent. Popular accounts call the astrolabe indispensable to
> navigation, but the Indian Ocean's own pilot literature is about star
> altitudes in fingers, bearings and seasons, and the European answer to the
> problem was to throw the astrolabe's machinery away rather than to carry it
> aboard — which is itself an argument. The mariner's astrolabe's origin date is
> unsettled, with claims running from Ramon Llull in 1295 to the late fifteenth
> century; the attribution to Martin Behaim rests on a late and doubted source;
> the earliest surviving *description* of making and using one is Martín Cortés
> de Albacar's *Arte de Navegar* of 1551. Counts of survivors differ because
> catalogues disagree about fragments and misattributions: 21 were known to
> Waters, 35 by 1979, 65 in Stimson's 1988 catalogue, about 108 by 2017, and a
> current inventory lists some 112 including parts. The Sodré instrument is a
> transitional object — a solid disc, uniquely marked at five degrees only, and
> uniquely bearing royal arms — so it should not be printed as a type specimen
> for the class. And individual astrolabes' dates and attributions are revised
> often enough that the handbook should cite a collection record for any single
> instrument rather than the phrase "the earliest astrolabe".

> **Sources:** Stimson, *The Mariner's Astrolabe: A Survey of Known, Surviving
> Sea Astrolabes* (1988); Mearns, Parham and Frohlich, "An Early Portuguese
> Mariner's Astrolabe from the Sodré Wreck-site, Al Hallaniyah, Oman",
> *International Journal of Nautical Archaeology* (2019); Tibbetts, *Arab
> Navigation in the Indian Ocean Before the Coming of the Portuguese* (1971),
> which translates Ibn Mājid's *Kitāb al-Fawāʾid* of 1490; King, *Islamic
> Astronomical Instruments* (1987), and *In Synchrony with the Heavens* (2004);
> Martín Cortés de Albacar, *Arte de Navegar* (1551); the al-Sabah Collection's
> record for the Nastulus astrolabe dated 315 AH (927/8 CE).

## The numbers behind it

No script for this one: every figure is a number of degrees times sixty, which
is the point worth making to the reader.

| Number | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| 1° of latitude | 60 nautical miles — the conversion the whole card turns on |
| 1°36′ (*isbaʿ*) | 96 nm: one finger-width on a kamal |
| 24′ (quarter *isbaʿ*) | 24 nm: the kamal's finest division |
| 5° | 300 nm: the Sodré astrolabe's scale interval |
| 1¼° | 75 nm: a quarter of that interval, read generously |
| 16′ | 16 nm: the Sun's semi-diameter, and so the error from sighting the wrong limb |
| 315 AH / 927–8 CE | the earliest dated surviving Islamic astrolabe |
| 1496–1501 | the Sodré astrolabe; the ship was lost in 1503 |
| 1490 | Ibn Mājid's *Kitāb al-Fawāʾid* |
| 1551 | the earliest surviving description of how to make and use a sea astrolabe |
| 21 → 35 → 65 → ~108 → ~112 | surviving mariner's astrolabes, as successive catalogues counted them |

## Notes for whoever draws the figure

**Draw the two instruments side by side at the same scale**, the planispheric
one with its plate and rete and the mariner's with its bare ring and bar, and
label the second "everything removed". The visual difference is the argument,
and no caption does it as quickly.

**If the figure shows a scale, show the Sodré spacing honestly** — eighteen
marks around a limb, not a finely divided circle. A reader who sees that five
degrees is the smallest mark will understand the rest of the chapter's
precision claims without being told.

**Do not draw a navigator using a planispheric astrolabe on a deck.** It is the
single most reproduced error in this subject, and the chapter can be the place
that stops repeating it.
