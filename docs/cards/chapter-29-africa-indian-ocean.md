# Chapter 29 — Across Cultures cards: African and Indian Ocean navigation

Handbook copy, written to Design's request of 5 November 2026. No course lesson
carries a card for this region — Lessons 8 and 18, Ibn Mājid and the kamal, are
spoken for by Chapter 27 — so the chapter had nothing labelled to build from.
Five cards follow, in the Chapter 26 form: label, paragraphs, the two Part VI
questions, a note on exchange, what remains uncertain, sources.

**Do not restate any claim in any of these cards without its label, its
uncertainty and its sources.**

A word on the chapter's own rule before the cards. The spec says not to present
these traditions as isolated where exchange is documented, and this is the one
chapter in Part VI where that instruction does real work: the Indian Ocean is
the best-documented case in the world of navigational knowledge crossing
cultures. Each card below ends with an exchange note saying who learned what
from whom, **as far as the evidence shows** — which in two cases is less than
the popular story claims, and in one case more.

### Across cultures — THE INDIAN OCEAN: A WIND WITH A TIMETABLE
**Evidence label: WELL DOCUMENTED**

The monsoon is the only wind system on earth that reverses on a schedule, and
that single fact organises everything else in this chapter. From roughly June to
September the south-west monsoon blows steadily towards India; from about
November to March the north-east monsoon blows back. A sailing passage across
the Arabian Sea is therefore not a question of *whether* you can get there but
of *when you may start* — and the penalty for missing the window is not a slower
passage, it is waiting half a year. The *Periplus of the Erythraean Sea*, a
Greek merchant's handbook of the first century CE, states the departure plainly:
ships for India leave Egypt about the month of July. Pliny, writing in the same
century, gives the passage as about forty days and the return on the winter
wind.

Put distances to it and the scale becomes clear. From the strait at Bab
el-Mandeb to Muziris on the Malabar coast is about **1,935 nautical miles** in a
straight line; the forty-day passage implies a made-good speed of about **48
nautical miles a day, two knots** — unimpressive hour by hour and
extraordinary as a system, because it is the same two knots in the same
direction for six weeks. That is what a trade wind buys: not speed, but the
abolition of the steering problem. The whole round trip is an annual cycle in
which a ship spends months in port waiting for the wind to turn, which is why
Indian Ocean ports developed resident foreign merchant communities and Atlantic
ones did not.

For the course, this is the cleanest case of a navigational problem solved by a
calendar rather than by an instrument. Lesson 26's argument is that longitude is
a clock problem; here is a sea where *the whole voyage* is a clock problem, and
the clock is the year. A navigator who knows the date knows the wind, and a
navigator who knows the wind and the latitude — Lesson 18's kamal, two fingers
of Polaris — has most of what the crossing requires.

**What stays still:** the calendar. The wind is the fixed reference, and it is
fixed in *time* rather than in space, which is unique in this book. **When it is
unavailable:** it never is, which is the point — but the inverse failure is
brutal. Sail late and the wind that would have carried you across becomes the
wind that pins you to a lee shore, and the loss is a season.

**On exchange:** the Greek story that a pilot named Hippalus "discovered" the
monsoon is a Greek story about a wind that Arab and Indian sailors were already
using; Casson's reading is that Greco-Roman use of the open-sea route developed
gradually from earlier voyages rather than from one man's insight. The *Periplus*
itself describes Arab and Indian shipping in the same ports its author traded
in. The honest sentence is that Rome joined a network, it did not open one.

> **What remains uncertain:** the *Periplus* is one merchant's practical
> handbook, not a survey — Casson dates it between about AD 40 and 70, and its
> coverage thins with distance from the Red Sea. The forty-day figure is
> Pliny's, for one route in one period, and should not be generalised into a
> crossing time. Monsoon onset dates vary by several weeks between years and
> between coasts, so the months above are a pattern and not a timetable in the
> railway sense. And the identification of ancient ports with modern places —
> Muziris above all — is argued, which affects any distance quoted from the
> text.

> **Sources:** Casson (translator and editor), *The Periplus Maris Erythraei*
> (1989); Pliny, *Natural History*, Book VI; Chaudhuri, *Trade and Civilisation
> in the Indian Ocean* (1985); Beaujard, *The Worlds of the Indian Ocean* (2019).

### Across cultures — THE SWAHILI COAST, ABOUT 800 TO 1500
**Evidence label: DOCUMENTED REGIONALLY**

The coast from Mogadishu to Kilwa is a chain of Muslim, Bantu-speaking
merchant towns — Lamu, Mombasa, Zanzibar, Kilwa and dozens more — whose
prosperity came from standing exactly where the monsoon stops. **Kilwa is the
limit of a one-season voyage**, and the gold of Sofala lies 728 nautical miles
further south: a ship that ran on past Kilwa could not get home before the wind
turned. So the gold came north in local craft and was transhipped, and the town
that owned the transhipment point owned the trade. The geography of wealth on
this coast is set by a wind calendar, not by a map.

The vessels are documented, and they are an Indian Ocean type rather than a
local curiosity. The *mtepe* was a sewn boat: planks drilled near their edges
and stitched together with three-strand coconut coir, pegged, and rigged with a
matting sail — no iron in the hull. Sewn hulls of this family were built all
around the western Indian Ocean, and on this coast the tradition survived into
living memory, the last vessels going in the Lamu archipelago in the mid-1930s,
with a reconstruction built in Zanzibar in 2003. How pilots navigated is
documented much more thinly than what they sailed: the practical literature of
this ocean that survives is Arabic — Ibn Mājid and Sulaymān al-Mahrī, Chapter 27's
material — while Swahili practice reaches us mainly through later ethnography
and through the seasons' own names in the language, *kaskazi* for the north-east
monsoon and *kusi* for the south-west.

The navigational point is one the course can test with its own arithmetic. A
kamal's finger-width is about 1°36′, which is **96 nautical miles of latitude**,
and the whole Swahili coast spans just **11° of latitude — seven finger-widths
end to end**. Latitude sailing cannot tell Malindi from Mombasa; it can only
tell you which third of the coast you are on. So this is a coast navigated the
way Lesson 30 says coasts are navigated: by landmark, by soundings, by knowing
which headland is which, and above all by carrying someone who has been there.
The instrument is the pilot.

**What stays still:** the coast itself, and the latitude of the ports to within
a finger. **When it is unavailable** — blown offshore, or caught out in the
turn of the monsoon — a crew has the latitude method and nothing else, which
puts them within a hundred miles of home and no closer.

**On exchange:** this is the card where exchange is the subject rather than a
footnote. Swahili is a Bantu language with heavy Arabic and Persian borrowing;
the towns are African towns in an Islamic trading world; the ships are an
Indian Ocean technology; the imported ceramics are Chinese and Persian. Do not
write this coast as Africa receiving navigation from Arabia. The documented
traffic runs in every direction, and the people doing the sailing were as often
Swahili as not.

> **What remains uncertain:** Swahili navigational technique specifically. We
> know the seasons, the ships and the ports; we do not have a Swahili pilot book
> to set beside the Arabic ones, and the ethnographic record was made in the
> twentieth century, when the trade had already changed. The claim that Swahili
> pilots used the kamal is reasonable and poorly evidenced — the instrument is
> documented for the ocean, not for the coast. Kilwa's chronology comes largely
> from one excavation programme and from a chronicle written later; the
> one-season limit is a well-attested pattern rather than a rule anybody wrote
> down. And "Swahili" covers dozens of towns that were frequently at war with
> each other; practice need not have been uniform.

> **Sources:** Chittick, *Kilwa: An Islamic Trading City on the East African
> Coast* (1974); Horton and Middleton, *The Swahili: The Social Landscape of a
> Mercantile Society* (2000); Prins, *Sailing from Lamu: A Study of Maritime
> Culture in Islamic East Africa* (1965); Ibn Baṭṭūṭa's account of his 1331
> visit to Mogadishu, Mombasa and Kilwa, in Gibb and Beckingham's translation
> for the Hakluyt Society (1958–1994); the *Periplus* (first century CE) for the
> earliest notice of the coast at Rhapta.

### Across cultures — THE SAHARA: SIJILMASA TO WALATA, 1352
**Evidence label: DOCUMENTED REGIONALLY**

We have an eyewitness, and he counted the days. Ibn Baṭṭūṭa left Sijilmasa with
a caravan on 1 Muḥarram 753 — 13 February 1352 — having bought camels and four
months' fodder. Twenty-five days to the salt mines at Taghaza, a place with no
inhabitants but the Masūfa who dug the salt, where buildings were made of salt
slabs roofed with camel skin and everything edible came in by caravan. Then the
stage that defines desert navigation: **ten nights to Walata with no water on
the way**, water carried from Taghaza for the whole of it. He reached Walata
"after a journey from Sijilmasa of two months to a day". As the crow flies that
is about **1,580 km in sixty days — 26 km a day**, and since no track is
straight, the real daily distance was higher.

What the sources actually describe is not star-steering but a system of people
and water. The caravan hired a **takshīf**: a man of the Masūfa sent ahead to
Walata with the travellers' letters, so that friends there would send out water
to meet the caravan four days' journey into the desert. Ibn Baṭṭūṭa says he paid
a hundred gold mithqāls for one, and notes drily that the takshīf often dies in
the desert, in which case Walata hears nothing and the caravan is not met. The
guides themselves are described as men who knew the route; what the texts do not
give is a technique — no star lore is recorded, no instrument, no method a
reader could follow. The desert's navigation, as the written sources have it, is
a hired memory with a water schedule attached.

For the course this is the limiting case of Lesson 28's distinction. There is no
measurement here at all: no latitude, no fix, nothing to check a position
against. Everything is carried — the route in a man's head, the water on the
camels — and when a carried quantity runs out in this terrain it does not
degrade, it kills. That is why the system's redundancy is social rather than
instrumental: a messenger sent ahead, a relief party sent out, prices that make
the risk worth running (a load of salt was worth eight to ten mithqāls at
Walata and twenty to thirty, sometimes forty, at Mali).

**What stays still:** the wells, and the men who know where they are. **When it
is unavailable:** nothing in the record replaces the guide. The modern
retellings in which Saharan travellers steer by stars and read dunes describe a
plausible practice that the medieval sources do not actually attest; a handbook
should say which it is printing.

**On exchange:** the trans-Saharan system ran on institutions as much as
camels — Islamic commercial law, written credit, a shared language of contract —
and the technical knowledge moved with the Masūfa and other Ṣanhāja Berber
groups who worked both ends of the route. The gold went north, the salt and the
books went south, and the guides were the part that could not be traded, only
hired.

> **What remains uncertain:** Ibn Baṭṭūṭa dictated his account years later,
> through a compiler, and some passages borrow from earlier writers; the day
> counts are his and should be quoted as his. Al-Bakrī's eleventh-century
> account describes routes he never travelled, from informants. Taghaza's and
> Walata's exact sites and the routes between them have shifted with the wells.
> And the absence of recorded star navigation is an absence in the written
> sources, which were written by travellers and not by guides — it is not
> evidence that the guides had no technique.

> **Sources:** Ibn Baṭṭūṭa, *Travels*, in Gibb and Beckingham's translation for
> the Hakluyt Society (1958–1994), volume IV for the Saharan crossing; al-Bakrī,
> *Kitāb al-Masālik wa-l-Mamālik* (1068), in Levtzion and Hopkins (eds),
> *Corpus of Early Arabic Sources for West African History* (1981); Lydon,
> *On Trans-Saharan Trails* (2009); Austen, *Trans-Saharan Africa in World
> History* (2010).

### Across cultures — MADAGASCAR: A SETTLEMENT DOCUMENTED, A VOYAGE NOT
**Evidence label: PLAUSIBLE BUT UNCERTAIN**

The label is on the voyage. That Madagascar was settled from island South-East
Asia is as well established as anything in this chapter: Malagasy is an
Austronesian language whose closest relatives are in the Barito group of
south-east Borneo; the crops excavated on the island are Asian — rice, mung
bean, cotton — where contemporary sites on the African mainland yield African
crops; and mitochondrial DNA points to a founding group of women so small it can
be counted, with one study's simulations putting it at about thirty, some
nine-tenths of Indonesian descent, around the ninth century. Three independent
lines of evidence — language, plants, genes — converge on the same answer.

None of them says anything whatever about how the crossing was navigated. The
distance from south-east Borneo to the northern tip of Madagascar is about
**3,913 nautical miles, 7,246 km** — at eighty miles a day, fifty days without
a stop, and that is the straight line across the open ocean rather than the
route anyone would have sailed. There is no text, no chart, no instrument, and
no oral tradition recorded early enough to carry weight. The crossing may have
been made in stages along the rim of the ocean, with the Comoros as the final
hinge; it may have been made by trading voyages that became a migration; the
"accidental drift voyage" version that circulates widely is one hypothesis
among several and is not supported by anything beyond the smallness of the
founding group.

The course's own discipline applies with unusual force here. Lesson 19 shows
that a star compass makes such a crossing conceivable; Lesson 24 shows that
*etak*-style reckoning makes it manageable; and neither is evidence that
anybody did it that way in the Indian Ocean. **A voyage that certainly happened
is not a technique that is documented**, and a handbook that fills the gap with
Pacific practice is quietly inventing the chapter's most interesting page.

**What stays still:** unknown. That is the honest entry. **When it is
unavailable:** also unknown — and the card should say so, because the two
blanks together are the finding.

**On exchange:** what *is* documented is the aftermath. Madagascar's population,
language, crops and livestock are Austronesian and African together, with Arab
and Indian contact layered on; the Comoros carry the same signature; the Asian
crops that arrive in East Africa arrive by the same connections. Whatever the
first crossing was, the island joined a network and the network reshaped it.

> **What remains uncertain:** nearly all the detail. The genetic estimate of
> about thirty founding women comes from simulations on mitochondrial DNA alone,
> so it describes the maternal line and not the size of a boat or a fleet, and
> it carries wide confidence limits. Dates differ by source: the archaeological
> crop evidence clusters in the eighth to tenth centuries, the genetic
> simulations around 830, and claims of much earlier human presence on the
> island — some based on cut-marked bones thousands of years older — are
> disputed and should not be mixed into this card. Whether the first arrivals
> came directly or by way of the African coast is open. And the ethnographic
> literature's Austronesian outrigger, which readers will expect to see, is a
> reasonable inference from technology elsewhere, not a find from Madagascar.

> **Sources:** Cox, Nelson, Tumonggor, Ricaut and Sudoyo, "A Small Cohort of
> Island Southeast Asian Women Founded Madagascar", *Proceedings of the Royal
> Society B* 279 (2012): 2761–2768; Crowther et al., "Ancient Crops Provide First
> Archaeological Signature of the Westward Austronesian Expansion", *PNAS* (2016);
> Adelaar, "The Indonesian Migrations to Madagascar" (2009), for the Barito
> linguistic argument; Beaujard, *The Worlds of the Indian Ocean* (2019).

### Across cultures — ZHENG HE'S FLEETS, 1405 TO 1433
**Evidence label: WELL DOCUMENTED**

Seven voyages in twenty-eight years, reaching Hormuz, the Red Sea and the East
African coast, and — unusually for this chapter — the navigation itself
survives on paper. The *Wubei Zhi*, a military encyclopaedia compiled in 1621,
prints a strip chart, originally about **20.5 cm by 560 cm** and cut into forty
pages for the book, running from Nanjing to Hormuz with the African coast drawn
along the bottom. It carries **499 place names**, of which Mills identified 423,
and Mogadishu, Barawa, Malindi and Mombasa are among them.

What makes it a navigation document rather than a picture is the sailing
directions written along the route: headings on a **24-point compass**, each
point subdivided into three, with distances given in *geng* — watches of **2.4
hours** — so that distance is expressed as time under sail, which silently takes
account of current and wind. Latitude is handled separately, by star altitude,
and four stellar diagrams at the end of the chart give the altitudes for two
passages: Sumatra to Sri Lanka, and Hormuz to Calicut, about **1,448 nautical
miles**. Altitudes are in finger-widths — *zhi*, quartered into *jiao* — read
with a graded board held at arm's length. This is Lesson 18's kamal with a
different handle: one finger is between 1.6° and 1.9° depending on which
authority you follow, which is to say **96 to 114 nautical miles of latitude**,
with the quarter-finger at 24 to 29.

So the chart records a complete practical system, and its limits are legible in
it. The scale is not constant — from about seven miles to the inch near Nanjing
to over two hundred along the African coast — the orientation changes within a
single page, and the further west it goes the thinner it gets. It is a book of
routes rather than a map of a sea: it tells you how to go from one place to the
next, and it cannot tell you where you are if you leave the route. That is
exactly the distinction Lesson 1 opens the course with, four hundred years
early and in Chinese.

**What stays still:** the stars for latitude, the compass for heading, and the
route itself, which is the thing the chart actually contains. **When it is
unavailable:** off the route the document is nearly useless, because its
geometry is not consistent enough to interpolate — a navigator blown off a
sailing direction has a compass and a star altitude and must find the route
again, not a position.

**On exchange:** this is the card where the documented exchange is *greater*
than the popular story. The star-altitude system is the Arab one in Chinese
units; the chart's place names include Arabic elements; Duyvendak and Pelliot
suggested it was partly based on Arab charts; and the fleets sailed a network of
pilots, ports and seasons that had been running for a millennium before they
arrived. Zheng He's fleets were enormous and they were visitors. The giraffe
that reached the Ming court came out of the same trade that brought Chinese
porcelain to Kilwa.

> **What remains uncertain:** the chart's date and authorship. It was printed in
> 1621 from material nearly two centuries older; Mills argued it was prepared
> for the sixth expedition and dates it about 1422, others put it between 1423
> and 1430, and no original survives. Some places are misplaced — Malindi
> appears south of Mombasa — and some known ports are missing, so it records
> practice imperfectly. The value of a *zhi* in degrees is given differently by
> different modern authorities, which is why this card prints a range rather
> than a figure. And the treasure-ship dimensions that dominate popular accounts
> are contested on structural and documentary grounds; they have nothing to do
> with navigation and the chapter does not need them.

> **Sources:** Mills (translator and editor), *Ma Huan: Ying-yai Sheng-lan, "The
> Overall Survey of the Ocean's Shores" (1433)*, Hakluyt Society (1970), which
> includes the chart and its sailing directions; Needham, *Science and
> Civilisation in China*, volume 4 part 3 (1971), for the compass and the
> star-altitude board; Dreyer, *Zheng He: China and the Oceans in the Early Ming
> Dynasty* (2007); Duyvendak, "The True Dates of the Chinese Maritime
> Expeditions in the Early Fifteenth Century" (1938).

## Where the evidence is too thin for a card

**A Swahili navigational technique of its own.** Said above and repeated here
because it is the chapter's most tempting gap to fill: the ships, seasons and
ports are documented; a Swahili method is not. Write the pilot, not the
instrument.

**Saharan star navigation.** Widely described in modern accounts, not attested
in the medieval sources this chapter cites. One sentence, flagged.

**How Madagascar was reached.** A whole card above exists to say this properly.

**African inland water navigation** — the Niger, the Congo, the Nile above the
cataracts — is real, important and almost entirely outside the sources this
chapter can cite responsibly. If the handbook wants it, it needs its own
research, not a paragraph borrowed from the trade-route literature.

## Notes for whoever draws the figures

**Draw the monsoon as a calendar, not as arrows on a map.** Two bars across a
twelve-month axis, one for each wind, with the *Periplus*'s July departure and
the return window marked — that shows the thing the chapter is actually about,
which is that the sea has opening hours.

**On the Swahili map, draw the seven finger-widths.** Latitude bands 1°36′ deep
down the coast, with Mogadishu, Malindi, Mombasa and Kilwa placed in them. A
reader sees instantly that three of those towns share a band, and the card's
argument needs no further words.

**For Madagascar, draw the three lines of evidence and leave the sea blank.**
Language, crops, genes, all converging on the island — and no track across the
water, because there is no track to draw. An empty ocean with a labelled blank
is an honest figure and a memorable one.

**For the Mao Kun chart, reproduce a strip, not a redrawn map.** Any modern
redrawing to consistent scale destroys the one thing worth showing: that the
document is a route and not a space.
