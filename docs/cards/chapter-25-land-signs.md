### Across cultures — THE PACIFIC: THE SIGNS THAT WIDEN AN ISLAND
**Evidence label: WELL DOCUMENTED**

Start with how small the target really is, because every sign in this section is
an answer to that number. Land itself is a few hundred yards of motu. What can
actually be seen from a canoe is the palms, and **"about ten miles" is a
statement about the palms rather than about the island**: with the eye a metre
and a half above the water and mature palms at 18 metres, the geometry puts them
on the horizon at 10.5 nautical miles, and the refraction allowance in the
nautical tables stretches that to 11.6. Shorter palms at 14 metres give 9.6 to
10.5. A bare sand cay with nothing growing on it gives **5.7 to 6.3** — barely
half. A high island of 300 metres gives 36 to 39. So a low atoll is a target
some twenty miles wide, and a navigator who has run two thousand miles cannot
rely on being within ten of anything.

The signs widen it, and they are not all worth the same. The most useful are the
**diurnal bird flights**, because a bird gives a direction and not merely a
presence: the noddy tern goes out to about 40 miles and the white tern to perhaps
120 — the Polynesian Voyaging Society publishes these as Nainoa Thompson's
estimates — and because they leave land in the morning and return at dusk, the
time of day tells you which way along the line to steer. The others are
presences: cloud piled and held over an island, the loom of a lagoon's white sand
and still water thrown up onto the underside of cloud, swells refracting around
land and reflecting back off it, drifting vegetation. Together they can turn a
twenty-mile atoll into something nearer eighty, which is a factor of four. But
the strategy that does the most work is not a sign at all, and sign-lists tend to
bury it: a navigator aims at a **screen** of islands rather than at one island.
On the Hawaiʻi-to-Tahiti passage the screen from Manihi to Maupiti is about 400
miles wide — five times what every bird and cloud in the chapter can add. Course
cross-reference: this is why Lesson 27 aims off deliberately and why the error
cone in Lesson 29 is drawn as wide as it is.

> **What remains uncertain:** the ranges are practitioners' estimates, not
> measurements, and they are honest about being so. Bird ranges vary with
> species, season, breeding state, weather and time of day; a lone bird means
> little where a large bird pile means a great deal; and the white tern's 120
> miles carries the Voyaging Society's own caveat that the bird may stay out
> overnight or come home unseen in the dark, which makes it a weaker sign than
> its number looks. The visibility figures above are geometric limits in clear
> air — whether a thin dark line is *noticed* at the horizon is a question of
> contrast and haze, and in practice sightings come later than the geometry
> allows, which is why the Voyaging Society's teaching pages say seven to ten
> miles rather than ten to twelve. Say which mile is meant: ten nautical miles is
> eleven and a half statute. **And te lapa — the flashes Lewis reported from
> Vaeakau-Taumako navigators as pointing towards land — must be printed as
> something reported and not as something a reader can use.** George's review of
> the candidate explanations (2012) found none of them sufficient; accounts
> disagree on whether the flashes are below the surface or on it; there is no
> instrumental record of the phenomenon at all. Finally, the signs in this
> section come from several traditions a thousand miles and more apart, and the
> chapter should not merge Micronesian and Polynesian practice into one Pacific
> method.

> **Sources:** Lewis, *We, the Navigators: The Ancient Art of Landfinding in the
> Pacific* (1972; 2nd edition 1994); Gladwin, *East Is a Big Bird: Navigation and
> Logic on Puluwat Atoll* (1970); Polynesian Voyaging Society, "How the Wayfinder
> Locates Land", its archived teaching pages, which carry Thompson's bird
> estimates and the screen-of-islands strategy (accessed 2026); George,
> "Polynesian Navigation and Te Lapa — 'The Flashing'", *Time and Mind* 5 (2012):
> 135–174; Bowditch, *The American Practical Navigator* (2019 edition) for the
> horizon rule.

## Where the visibility numbers come from

An object of height *h* meets the horizon of an eye at height *e* at a distance

    d = k · (√e + √h)        d in nautical miles, e and h in feet

with *k* = 1.064 for the geometry alone — it is √(2R) with R = 3,440 nautical
miles, converted — and *k* = 1.17 for the standard refraction allowance in the
nautical tables. Both are printed in the card because the difference between
them, about a mile, is smaller than the difference between tall palms and short
ones, and much smaller than the difference between palms and bare sand. That is
the point worth making to a reader: **the ten-mile figure is set by vegetation
height, and the honest version of the sentence names it.**
