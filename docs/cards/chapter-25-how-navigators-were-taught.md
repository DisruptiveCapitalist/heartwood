### Across cultures — THE CENTRAL CAROLINE ISLANDS, 1950 TO THE PRESENT
**Evidence label: WELL DOCUMENTED**

What the record shows is not a folk memory passed down vaguely but **a school
system**. In the central Carolines two schools of navigation survived into the
twentieth century — Weriyeng and Fanur — teaching by apprenticeship inside
lineages, orally, by rote and by chant, with the star compass laid out on the
ground in shells or stones to be learned by heart and recited back. The knowledge
was owned. It was given with obligations attached, in graded stages: the
Polynesian Voyaging Society's account of the ceremony it took part in names
**pwo** as the ninth of fifteen degrees in Weriyeng. Pwo is the initiation, and
the dates matter because they show how close the thing came to stopping. Mau
Piailug went through pwo on Satawal at about twenty, around 1951. The next pwo
held on Satawal was on **18 March 2007**, when he inducted five Hawaiians and
eleven Micronesians — some five decades later, and his own teachers were gone.

The second half of this section is the part a reader can watch happening, and it
is the best-documented transmission of navigational knowledge anywhere in the
world. In 1976 Hōkūleʻa sailed from Hawaiʻi to Tahiti navigated by Mau, because
no navigator was left in Hawaiʻi to do it; from 1979 he taught Nainoa Thompson;
in 1980 Thompson navigated to Tahiti and back without instruments, which had not
been done from Hawaiʻi in roughly six centuries. Two things about that are worth
saying plainly in a handbook. **What came out of it is a new system built on
Carolinian teaching, not a recovered Hawaiian one** — the Hawaiian star compass
in use today is Thompson's own construction, and it is better for being
described as his work. And **the tradition is not a historical topic**: in August
2026 the Voyaging Society inducted six new pwo navigators in Hawaiʻi, in a
ceremony officiated by Satawalese pwo navigators and the first of its kind held
there. As it was explained to Thompson, pwo is a matter of light, love, kindness
and compassion — an obligation towards one's people. It is not a qualification,
and a chapter that presents it as the Pacific equivalent of a master's ticket has
mistaken what it is for.

> **What remains uncertain:** the record is narrow and late. Nearly all of it is
> Puluwat and Satawal between the 1960s and the 1980s, written by outsiders —
> Gladwin, Lewis and Thomas, each of whom apprenticed himself to a navigator — at
> a time when the practice had already contracted to a handful of men, so what
> was recorded is the end of a tradition rather than the tradition at work.
> Accounts of the grades and of what each contains differ, and the published
> figures disagree even on the arithmetic around them: the gap before the 2007
> ceremony is given as fifty years in one account and fifty-six in another, and
> the year of Mau's own initiation varies between sources. Some of this knowledge
> is restricted and was deliberately not published; a chapter should not
> reconstruct from fragments what was withheld on purpose. Most important:
> **none of this is evidence about how Polynesian navigators were taught before
> contact.** There is no comparable record for Polynesia. The teaching that was
> documented is Micronesian, the Hawaiian revival learned it from a Micronesian
> navigator rather than remembering its own, and treating the fifteen degrees of
> Weriyeng as a Pacific-wide system would be exactly the error this card exists
> to prevent.

> **Sources:** Gladwin, *East Is a Big Bird: Navigation and Logic on Puluwat
> Atoll* (1970); Lewis, *We, the Navigators* (1972; 2nd edition 1994); Thomas,
> *The Last Navigator* (1987), for Mau Piailug's own initiation; Finney, *Voyage
> of Rediscovery* (1994); Polynesian Voyaging Society, its history pages on the
> Satawal pwo of 18 March 2007 and on the Hawaiʻi induction of 2026 (accessed
> 2026).

## A note for whoever draws the figure

If the section is illustrated, the thing to draw is the chain of people — Mau's
teachers, Mau, the five of 2007, the six of 2026 — and not a diagram of fifteen
grades. The grades are the part the sources disagree about; the chain is the part
they agree on, and it is also the argument.
