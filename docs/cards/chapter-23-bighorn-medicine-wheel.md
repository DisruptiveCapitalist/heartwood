### Across cultures — MEDICINE MOUNTAIN, BIGHORN NATIONAL FOREST, WYOMING
**Evidence label: MODERN INTERPRETATION**

The label is on the astronomy, not on the site. The wheel itself is thoroughly
documented, and it is in use: a rim of local limestone about 82 feet across, 28
spokes running in to a hollow central cairn some 12 feet wide, six more cairns
at or near the rim, at 9,640 feet on the north-west ridge of Medicine Mountain,
inside a National Historic Landmark of 4,080 acres. It is an active Native
American sacred site, used by many nations and managed under a preservation plan
whose signatories include two tribal organisations alongside the Forest Service
and the State Historic Preservation Office. No nation has publicly claimed to
have built it, and Crow accounts given during the landmark negotiations say it
was already there when they came into the country. The one reliable scientific
date is a single piece of wood from the western cairn whose last growth ring is
1760 CE — a minimum age for that piece of wood, not a date for the structure,
which is thought to be composite, its central and outer cairns older than its
rim and spokes. Published age estimates run from a few hundred years to more
than three thousand; the mountain has been visited for nearly seven thousand.

What the label is on is the reading of the stones as an instrument. In 1974 the
astronomer John Eddy reported that one pair of cairns lines up with sunrise at
the June solstice and another with sunset, and that three further pairs point at
the places where Sirius, Aldebaran and Rigel make their first appearance of the
year before dawn; a later proposal added Fomalhaut on a fifth pair. Read that
way, the wheel is a calendar. The reason to hold the reading at arm's length is
not that the survey was careless — it was not — but that the stones cannot carry
the precision the claim needs, and this is arithmetic a reader can do. The
sightlines are short: the rim is 82 feet across, so a line from a rim cairn to
the centre is about 41 feet long, drawn between two piles of rock several feet
wide. Fix each pile's centre to within half a metre and the direction of that
line is uncertain by **a little over two degrees**; sight on the edges of the
stones instead and you have a dozen degrees of slack. Now ask what two degrees
is worth as a calendar. At this latitude the Sun's rising point barely moves
near the solstice: **one degree of error is a fortnight either side of it, two
degrees about nineteen days, three degrees about three and a half weeks** — and
those figures hardly change if you allow for the mountain horizon. The
instrument is blunt exactly where the claim needs it sharp. Second piece of
arithmetic, and the one the critics rest on: seven cairns make 21 pairs, which
is 42 directions; add the 28 spokes and the wheel offers something like a
hundred candidate lines, one every three or four degrees right round the
horizon. A match within a couple of degrees of a bright star's rising place is
then not a discovery but what chance alone supplies.

> **What remains uncertain:** whether the wheel was built, or used, with any
> astronomical intent at all. The reference survey of the question, in the
> *Handbook of Archaeoastronomy and Ethnoastronomy*, treats the early
> "observatory" readings as unfounded and the attempts to decode these
> structures as unfulfilled and possibly misguided; a 1987 re-examination in
> *Plains Anthropologist* had already argued that the alignments are no better
> than chance would give and that solstice sightlines of this kind cannot follow
> the Sun's declination with any accuracy. Note also that the dates offered for
> the alignments — AD 1200 to 1700 for the Aldebaran line, AD 1050 to 1450 for
> Fomalhaut — are *derived from* the alignments by precession, so they are not
> independent evidence that the wheel is of that age. Nothing in the
> ethnographic record describes the wheel being used as a sighting instrument or
> for finding a way. What the ethnography does record — the Forest Service
> summarises Boggs's fieldwork of the 1990s — is a landscape with distinct
> places for approach, ceremony, prayer and vision questing, the wheel itself
> described as the altar of the complex, and major sacred sites standing as the
> fixed points of an oriented world by which people placed their movements.
> That is a navigational role, and a better documented one than the sightlines;
> it is the one a reader should take from this card. Finally, "medicine wheel"
> is a modern umbrella term covering dozens of structures of unlike design — a
> 1988 appraisal classified sixty-seven of them — so a reading of this one does
> not transfer to the others, and none of them should be described as a type.
> **Do not describe the nations who hold Medicine Mountain sacred as one
> culture, and do not write the wheel up as a puzzle solved by an astronomer.**

> **Sources:** Eddy, "Astronomical Alignment of the Big Horn Medicine Wheel",

## Where the two numbers come from

Both are the reader's to check, so here is the working. Site latitude
44° 49′ 34″ N = 44.826°, from the landmark's own coordinate.

**The sightline's slack.** Two marks whose positions are known to ±*w* at a
separation *d* define a direction to about ±arctan(*w*/*d*). The rim is 82 feet
across, so a rim-to-centre line is 41 feet = 12.5 m. With *w* = 0.5 m that is
2.3°. Using the stones as seen rather than their centres — a 12-foot central
cairn and a 6-foot rim cairn — gives arctan((1.8 + 0.9)/12.5) = 12.4°. A longer
baseline helps only slowly: 60 feet with the same half-metre gives 1.6°.

**What a degree is worth near the solstice.** For a body of declination δ rising
to a horizon of altitude *h* at latitude φ,

    cos A = (sin δ − sin h · sin φ) / (cos h · cos φ)

with A measured from north. Taking δ(d) = 23.44° · cos(0.9856° · d) for *d* days
from the June solstice, the azimuth of sunrise moves away from its solstice
value by 0.5° after 10 days, 1° after 14, 2° after 19 and 3° after 24. Those
day-counts are the same to within a day for horizon altitudes from −0.5° to +2°,
which is why the mountain horizon does not rescue the claim: the Sun's rising
point is simply slow near the solstice, which is what makes a solstice easy to
recognise and hard to date.

**The count of candidate lines.** Seven cairns give C(7,2) = 21 pairs and so 42
directed azimuths, a mean gap of 8.6°. The 28 spokes add 56 more; about a
hundred in all, a mean gap of 3.7°.

## A note for whoever draws the figure

If the chapter carries a diagram of the wheel, draw the cairns at their real
size. Every published figure that draws them as points makes the sightlines look
like instrument lines, and that single choice of symbol is most of the argument
the chapter is trying to weigh.
