HEARTWOOD UNIVERSITY
The Human Body
Anatomy, Physiology, and the Science of Health
Complete Course-Build Specification for Claude Code

1. THE MISSION
Build a complete six-week, 30-lesson Heartwood University course titled:
The Human Body: Anatomy, Physiology, and the Science of Health
Subtitle: From the Cell to the Whole Person.
This course is designed for an adult learner with curiosity about the human body, health, disease, medicines, aging, and peptides, but without requiring a background in biology or medicine.
The learner wants to understand how the human body works, not simply memorize anatomical terms.
The course must answer questions such as:
- What is the human body made of?
- What are atoms, molecules, cells, tissues, organs, and organ systems?
- How do cells work, and why are mitochondria important?
- How does the body turn food into usable energy?
- What are proteins, peptides, hormones, and receptors?
- How does the heart circulate blood?
- Why do our hands and feet sometimes become cold?
- How do bones develop, what are they made of, and why do they change with age?
- How do muscles produce movement?
- How does the body recognize infection and fight disease?
- What is inflammation, and when is it helpful or harmful?
- What is cancer, and how does it begin?
- How do the male and female reproductive systems work?
- How do medicines travel through the body and produce their effects?
- Why can a substance help one person but harm another?
- How do different organs communicate and work together?
- How can a person evaluate claims about peptides, supplements, treatments, and health products?
The learner should finish the course with a connected understanding of the human body and a stronger ability to ask informed questions about health.
The central promise of Heartwood: understanding before memorization.

3. THE ESTABLISHED HEARTWOOD LEARNING EXPERIENCE
Use the same structure as Physics I:
- Six weeks.
- Thirty lessons.
- Five lessons per week.
- Approximately 30 minutes per lesson.
- Beginner-friendly instruction.
- No prerequisites.
- No grades or intimidating examinations.
- Progress measured by developing understanding and practical application.
Every lesson must use the established six-stage cycle:
- SEE IT
- UNDERSTAND IT
- CONNECT IT
- FEYNMAN IT
- CHALLENGE IT
- REFLECT
Preserve the established tutor roles:
- THE PROFESSOR — explains the underlying science accurately.
- THE ROOMMATE — makes the explanation conversational and relatable.
- THE EDITOR — identifies the central idea and removes unnecessary complexity.
- THE BUILDER — connects the science to physical structures, body systems, and practical examples.
- FEYNMAN CHECK — invites the learner to explain an idea in their own words.
- THE JOURNAL — encourages reflection and recognition of progress.
Preserve the existing “I don't get it” routes:
- Analogy.
- Object.
- Story.
- Experiment.
- Smaller question.
- Backward explanation.
Preserve the Understanding Map:
Encountered → Recognize → Understand → Explain → Use
Do not introduce grades, scores, streak pressure, or a separate medical-testing system.
The learner should feel that a patient, knowledgeable teacher is helping her understand something she has always wondered about.
4. TEACHING PHILOSOPHY
Begin with a question or an everyday experience, not a textbook definition.
For example, instead of beginning with the definition of a mitochondrion, ask:
“Your muscles, brain, and heart all need energy every minute. Where does that energy come from, and how does a cell make it available?”
Instead of beginning with vasoconstriction, ask:
“Why might your fingers become cold even when the rest of your body feels reasonably warm?”
Instead of beginning with a definition of cancer, ask:
“Your body replaces old cells with new ones all the time. What happens when a cell stops following the rules?”
The explanation should unfold gradually:
- Establish curiosity.
- Introduce a concrete example.
- Explain the mechanism in everyday language.
- Introduce the proper scientific vocabulary.
- Connect the idea to another body system.
- Ask the learner to explain it.
- Explore an application or challenge.
- Reflect on what has become clearer.
Use analogies, but explain where each analogy stops being accurate.
Introduce terminology in manageable amounts. Define abbreviations when first used.
When scientific detail becomes complex, simplify the explanation without making it misleading.
The course should emphasize relationships and cause and effect. A learner should begin to see how a molecule can influence a cell, how cells form tissues, how tissues support organs, and how organs work together to maintain the body.
5. THE SIX-WEEK CURRICULUM
WEEK 1 — THE AMAZING WORLD INSIDE US
From atoms to living cells
Lesson 1 — What Are We Made Of?
Begin with the question: What is a human being made of at the smallest levels?
Introduce atoms, elements, molecules, water, and the major elements found in the body. Explain the roles of carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, phosphorus, calcium, sodium, potassium, and iron.
Show how the same elements can form very different substances depending on how their atoms are arranged.
Introduce the hierarchy:
Atoms → Molecules → Cells → Tissues → Organs → Organ Systems → Organism.
Clarify that living matter follows the same physical and chemical principles as the rest of nature, while living systems organize those processes in remarkable ways.
Lesson 2 — The Cell: A Tiny Living World
Explain the cell as the basic structural and functional unit of the body.
Introduce the cell membrane, cytoplasm, nucleus, DNA, ribosomes, endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus, lysosomes, and mitochondria.
Do not teach every organelle as a vocabulary item. Explain what the cell needs to accomplish and how its structures contribute.
Use a visual cutaway diagram and follow a simple example of a cell receiving a signal and producing a protein.
Lesson 3 — Mitochondria and the Energy of Life
Explore how cells make usable energy.
Introduce nutrients, glucose, oxygen, cellular respiration, ATP (adenosine triphosphate), and the role of mitochondria.
Explain ATP as a molecule cells use to transfer energy to processes that need it.
Clarify that mitochondria are central to aerobic energy production, but not all cellular ATP production occurs in mitochondria, and not every cell relies on them in the same way.
Connect cellular energy to muscle contraction, nerve signaling, active transport, and other cellular work.
Ask: Why does a working muscle need more energy than a resting muscle?
Lesson 4 — Proteins, Peptides, and DNA
Introduce DNA as the cell's genetic information, genes as sections of that information, and proteins as molecules built from amino acids.
Explain transcription and translation at a conceptual level: how information in DNA can be used to make RNA and then a protein.
Distinguish:
- Amino acids: building blocks.
- Peptides: chains of amino acids, often relatively short.
- Proteins: one or more amino-acid chains folded into structures with particular functions.
Explain that there is no universal length boundary separating every peptide from every protein.
Introduce enzymes, structural proteins, transport proteins, and signaling molecules.
Establish an early foundation for understanding collagen, hormones, receptors, and peptide-based medicines.
Lesson 5 — How Cells Communicate and Become Tissues
Explain how cells receive signals, recognize molecules, and respond.
Introduce receptors, chemical messengers, cell specialization, and the formation of tissues.
Explain the four broad tissue categories: epithelial, connective, muscle, and nervous tissue.
Introduce the concept of homeostasis: the body's continuous regulation of internal conditions within workable ranges.
Use examples such as temperature regulation and blood glucose control.
Week 1 outcome
The learner can explain the basic organization of the body, the functions of major cell structures, how cells obtain usable energy, and the difference between amino acids, peptides, and proteins.

WEEK 2 — THE BODY'S TRANSPORTATION AND CONTROL SYSTEMS
Blood, heart, nerves, and temperature
Lesson 6 — The Heart: More Than a Pump
Explain the heart's chambers, valves, and coordinated contractions.
Trace blood through the right side of the heart to the lungs and back, then through the left side to the body.
Introduce the distinction between pulmonary and systemic circulation.
Explain heart rate, stroke volume, and cardiac output in simple terms.
Show how the heart's electrical conduction system coordinates contractions.
Lesson 7 — Blood, Arteries, Veins, and Capillaries
Explain the functions of red blood cells, white blood cells, platelets, and plasma.
Compare arteries, veins, and capillaries by their structure and function.
Explain how oxygen, nutrients, hormones, waste products, and immune cells travel through the body.
Clarify that arteries carry blood away from the heart and veins carry blood toward it; these definitions are based on direction, not oxygen content.
Introduce blood pressure and explain why circulation requires both pressure and appropriately regulated vessels.
Lesson 8 — Oxygen, the Lungs, and Cellular Needs
Trace oxygen from the air into the lungs, across the alveoli, into the blood, and to body tissues.
Explain gas exchange, hemoglobin, and carbon dioxide removal.
Show how the respiratory and circulatory systems cooperate.
Introduce the idea that adequate oxygen delivery depends on more than breathing alone: circulation, blood composition, and tissue demand also matter.
Lesson 9 — Why Do Hands and Feet Get Cold?
Use cold hands and feet as the opening mystery.
Explain the body's response to cold, including vasoconstriction, changes in blood flow to the skin, and preservation of core temperature.
Distinguish the body's temperature-regulation mechanisms from conditions that can affect circulation or sensation.
Introduce the possible roles of the environment, individual physiology, blood vessels, nerves, and other factors.
Explain that persistent, painful, asymmetric, discolored, or otherwise concerning symptoms can warrant medical evaluation. Do not diagnose the learner from a single symptom.
Lesson 10 — The Nervous System: Signals at Extraordinary Speed
Explain the brain, spinal cord, peripheral nerves, and neurons.
Introduce electrical signaling in nerve cells and chemical communication across synapses.
Explain sensory input, processing, and motor output.
Compare nervous-system signaling with hormone signaling: nervous signals are often rapid and targeted, while hormonal signals may act more slowly and persist longer, although there are exceptions.
Connect the nervous system to muscle movement, sensation, temperature regulation, and organ control.
Week 2 outcome
The learner can trace blood through the heart and lungs, explain how tissues receive oxygen, understand the basic roles of nerves and hormones, and explain why blood flow to the skin changes with temperature.

WEEK 3 — BONES, MUSCLES, AND MOVEMENT
The living framework of the body
Lesson 11 — Bones Are Living Tissue
Explain the functions of the skeleton: support, protection, movement, mineral storage, and blood-cell production in bone marrow.
Introduce compact bone, spongy bone, osteoblasts, osteoclasts, and osteocytes.
Explain that bones continually remodel in response to mechanical forces, hormones, age, nutrition, and other influences.
Clarify that bones are living organs, not inert structural material.
Lesson 12 — What Are Bones Made Of?
Explore the combination of collagen, mineral crystals, water, and living cells that gives bone its properties.
Explain collagen as a protein that contributes to flexibility and structural organization, while mineral content contributes greatly to stiffness and compressive strength.
Introduce calcium and phosphate in bone mineral.
Explain that bone health depends on multiple interacting factors, including nutrition, hormones, physical activity, genetics, age, and certain medical conditions.
Discuss osteoporosis conceptually without prescribing supplements or treatment.
Lesson 13 — Joints, Cartilage, Tendons, and Ligaments
Explain how bones meet at joints and how joint structures support movement.
Distinguish:
- Cartilage: provides cushioning and smooth joint surfaces in relevant locations.
- Tendons: connect muscles to bones or other structures.
- Ligaments: connect bones and help stabilize joints.
- Synovial fluid: helps lubricate synovial joints.
Explain that different joints are built for different combinations of stability and movement.
Use the knee, shoulder, hip, and spine as examples.
Lesson 14 — The Major Muscles and How They Work
Introduce skeletal muscle, smooth muscle, and cardiac muscle.
Explore major skeletal muscle groups and their roles in movement and posture.
Explain how muscle fibers generate force, how nerves activate muscle, and how actin and myosin interact to produce contraction.
Introduce the idea that muscles often work in coordinated groups, with some muscles producing movement while others stabilize a joint.
Explain that strength, endurance, flexibility, and muscle size are related but distinct qualities.
Lesson 15 — Movement, Recovery, and Aging
Bring bones, joints, muscles, nerves, and circulation together.
Explain how physical activity places demands on the body and how tissues adapt over time.
Discuss muscle repair, bone remodeling, connective tissue, rest, nutrition, and age-related changes.
Distinguish ordinary recovery from injury or disease.
Introduce the concept of progressive loading and explain why a body needs an appropriate balance between activity and recovery.
Do not provide individualized exercise prescriptions or imply that one routine is suitable for everyone.
Week 3 outcome
The learner can explain how bones are structured, why collagen and minerals both matter, how muscles contract, and how the skeletal and muscular systems cooperate to produce movement.

WEEK 4 — THE BODY'S CHEMICAL MESSENGERS
Hormones, receptors, metabolism, and peptides
Lesson 16 — The Endocrine System: Messages Through the Body
Introduce the pituitary, thyroid, parathyroid glands, adrenal glands, pancreas, ovaries, and testes.
Explain that endocrine glands release hormones into circulation, allowing them to influence cells with the appropriate receptors.
Introduce negative feedback using a familiar example such as blood glucose regulation or thyroid hormone regulation.
Explain that hormones interact with other systems rather than acting independently.
Lesson 17 — Hormones and Peptides: Similarities and Differences
Explore the distinction between hormones and peptides.
Explain that some hormones are peptides or proteins, while others are steroid hormones or molecules derived from amino acids.
Use insulin as a well-established example of a peptide hormone.
Explain how insulin interacts with receptors and influences glucose handling in target tissues.
Clarify that not every peptide is a hormone, not every hormone is a peptide, and not every peptide sold as a health product has a proven therapeutic effect.
Lesson 18 — Receptors: How Cells Receive Instructions
Explain receptors as molecules that recognize particular signals and initiate cellular responses.
Introduce the idea of molecular shape, binding, signaling pathways, and changes in cell behavior.
Compare receptor-mediated signaling to receiving a message and activating a response, while clarifying that real biological signaling is more complex than a simple lock-and-key metaphor.
Explain why a molecule's ability to bind a receptor does not by itself prove a meaningful clinical benefit.
Introduce the importance of dose, route of administration, target tissue, duration of exposure, and biological context.
Lesson 19 — Metabolism, Insulin, and Energy Balance
Explain metabolism as the collection of chemical processes that sustain life.
Explore how the body uses, stores, and mobilizes energy from carbohydrates, fats, and proteins.
Explain the roles of insulin and other metabolic signals without reducing body weight or metabolic health to a single hormone.
Introduce the liver, pancreas, skeletal muscle, and adipose tissue as cooperating organs.
Explain that metabolic regulation is influenced by multiple signals, genetics, food intake, activity, sleep, illness, and other factors.
Lesson 20 — Peptides in Health: Evidence, Possibilities, and Risks
Use the concepts from the previous four lessons to build a framework for evaluating peptide claims.
Distinguish among:
- Peptides naturally produced by the body.
- Peptide medicines with established, specific indications.
- Products being investigated in clinical research.
- Compounded preparations.
- Products sold for research use or other purposes that may not be approved for human treatment.
Explain that these categories are not interchangeable.
Teach the learner to ask:
- What molecule is actually being discussed?
- What is its proposed biological target?
- What evidence exists in cells, animals, and humans?
- Have meaningful clinical benefits been demonstrated?
- What adverse effects, interactions, and uncertainties are known?
- Is the product approved for the intended use?
- How reliable are the source, manufacturing, purity, and storage conditions?
Explain that a promising biological mechanism does not guarantee safety or effectiveness in people.
Do not recommend unapproved peptide use, dosing, injection instructions, or treatment changes. Encourage consultation with an appropriately qualified clinician and pharmacist for individual treatment questions.
Week 4 outcome
The learner can explain the roles of hormones and receptors, distinguish peptides from other biological signals, and evaluate health-related peptide claims using evidence rather than marketing language.

WEEK 5 — DEFENSE, REPAIR, DISEASE, AND CANCER
How the body protects itself—and what can go wrong
Lesson 21 — The Immune System: Recognizing a Threat
Introduce physical barriers, innate immunity, adaptive immunity, white blood cells, antibodies, and immune memory.
Explain how the immune system recognizes patterns associated with threats and responds to infection.
Distinguish innate immune responses from adaptive responses without suggesting that the two systems operate independently.
Use an illustrative infection to show how multiple defenses cooperate.
Lesson 22 — Inflammation, Fever, and Healing
Explain inflammation as a coordinated response to injury, infection, or other triggers.
Introduce redness, heat, swelling, and pain as common features of inflammation, while explaining that internal inflammation may not be visibly obvious.
Explain fever as a regulated change in the body's temperature set point during certain illnesses.
Explore tissue repair, clotting, immune-cell activity, and rebuilding.
Explain that inflammation can be protective in the short term but harmful when excessive, misdirected, or persistent.
Do not imply that inflammation can always be judged from symptoms alone.
Lesson 23 — Infection, Disease, and the Body's Response
Explain the differences among infection, disease, symptoms, and risk factors.
Introduce bacteria, viruses, fungi, and parasites at a beginner-friendly level.
Explain that pathogens differ in how they enter the body, reproduce, spread, and evade defenses.
Explore how vaccines, antimicrobial medicines, and supportive care can serve different roles, while noting that treatment depends on the specific illness.
Explain that antibiotics act against susceptible bacteria, not viruses.
Teach the learner to distinguish general biological explanations from personal medical advice.
Lesson 24 — Cancer: When Cells Stop Following the Rules
Explain normal cell division, DNA damage, mutations, growth regulation, and programmed cell death.
Describe cancer as a group of diseases in which abnormal cells acquire the ability to grow and survive inappropriately, with some cancers invading surrounding tissues or spreading to distant sites.
Introduce oncogenes, tumor-suppressor genes, and DNA repair at a conceptual level.
Explain that cancer develops through complex combinations of biological changes, environmental exposures, inherited susceptibility, aging, and chance. Causes and risk factors differ among cancers.
Clarify that not every tumor is cancerous and that not every cancer behaves the same way.
Introduce the difference between localized disease and metastasis.
Lesson 25 — How the Body Detects and Responds to Abnormal Cells
Explore how immune surveillance, tissue environments, blood supply, and cell signaling relate to cancer development.
Explain that cancer cells may evade immune responses and that tumors can interact with surrounding tissues in complex ways.
Introduce the broad purposes of surgery, radiation therapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapies, immunotherapy, and hormone therapy.
Explain that treatment choices depend on cancer type, stage, molecular characteristics, overall health, and other factors.
Avoid presenting cancer as simply a failure of the immune system or suggesting that one supplement or peptide can prevent or cure it.
Week 5 outcome
The learner can explain how immune defenses work, why inflammation occurs, how infections differ, and how cancer develops through changes in normal cellular control.

WEEK 6 — REPRODUCTION, MEDICINES, AND THE WHOLE BODY
Understanding the body as an interconnected system
Lesson 26 — The Female Reproductive System
Introduce the ovaries, fallopian tubes, uterus, cervix, vagina, and external reproductive structures.
Explain ovulation, the menstrual cycle, the roles of estrogen and progesterone, and how reproductive hormones interact through feedback mechanisms.
Discuss menopause and the changes associated with declining ovarian hormone production.
Keep the explanation respectful, scientifically accurate, and appropriate for an adult learner.
Explain normal variation and distinguish general education from individual medical assessment.
Lesson 27 — The Male Reproductive System and Human Reproduction
Introduce the testes, epididymis, vas deferens, seminal vesicles, prostate, penis, and related structures.
Explain sperm production, testosterone, and the hormonal regulation of reproductive function.
Show how the male and female reproductive systems contribute to fertilization and reproduction.
Explain that reproductive hormones have effects beyond reproduction, including effects on bone, muscle, blood production, and other tissues.
Lesson 28 — How Medicines Work in the Body
Explain pharmacology at a beginner-friendly level.
Introduce:
- Absorption: how a substance enters the bloodstream or reaches a target site.
- Distribution: how it moves through the body.
- Metabolism: how the body chemically transforms it.
- Excretion: how the body removes it.
- Receptors and other molecular targets.
- Dose-response relationships.
- Intended effects, adverse effects, and drug interactions.
Explain why the same dose can affect different people differently.
Discuss the influence of age, kidney and liver function, other medicines, genetics, and the route of administration.
Distinguish a medicine's mechanism of action from evidence that it improves a meaningful health outcome.
Lesson 29 — The Digestive System, Liver, and Kidneys
Explore how food is broken down, nutrients are absorbed, and waste is removed.
Introduce the mouth, stomach, intestines, liver, pancreas, and kidneys.
Explain the liver's roles in metabolism and chemical processing, and the kidneys' roles in filtering blood, regulating fluid and electrolytes, and removing certain waste products.
Connect digestion, circulation, cellular energy, and medicine processing.
Explain that these organs interact, and that a problem in one system can affect others.
Lesson 30 — The Whole Person: Connecting the Systems
Bring together the concepts from all six weeks.
Present several educational scenarios, such as:
- A person exercises and the muscles need more oxygen and energy.
- A person becomes cold and blood flow to the skin changes.
- An infection activates immune responses and inflammation.
- A bone undergoes continuous remodeling.
- A hormone binds to a receptor and changes a cell's behavior.
- A medicine is absorbed, processed, and eliminated.
- An abnormal cell acquires changes that allow uncontrolled growth.
Ask the learner to trace the chain of events across cells, tissues, organs, and systems.
End with a personal reflection:
“What do you understand about the human body now that you could not have explained six weeks ago?”
Invite the learner to choose a subject for deeper study, such as cellular energy, bone biology, hormones, peptides, immune function, or pharmacology.
Week 6 outcome
The learner can describe the major reproductive organs, explain how medicines act and are processed, and connect multiple body systems into a coherent explanation of health and disease.

7. THE SIX-STAGE LESSON TEMPLATE
Every lesson must contain substantive content in all six stages.
SEE IT
Open with a question, observation, or everyday mystery.
Examples:
- Why does a muscle need oxygen?
- Why are bones strong but not completely rigid?
- How can a hormone influence a distant organ?
- How does a cell recognize a signal?
- Why does a fever occur?
- How can one abnormal cell eventually form a tumor?
UNDERSTAND IT
Explain the core mechanism in plain language before introducing detailed terminology.
Use a visual or analogy where helpful. Then introduce the proper scientific explanation.
Distinguish what is well established from what remains uncertain.
CONNECT IT
Link the current lesson to earlier concepts and other body systems.
Examples:
- Connect mitochondria to muscle contraction and nerve activity.
- Connect collagen to bone, tendons, skin, and other connective tissues.
- Connect circulation to oxygen delivery, immune responses, and medicine distribution.
- Connect receptors to hormones, peptides, and pharmacology.
- Connect DNA and cell division to cancer.
FEYNMAN IT
Ask the learner to explain the central idea in her own words.
Use prompts such as:
- “Explain this to a friend who has never studied biology.”
- “What is the main job of this structure?”
- “How does this process begin, and what happens next?”
- “What would happen if this part stopped functioning normally?”
- “How would you distinguish these two concepts?”
Respond to the learner's actual explanation. Identify what she understands, correct misconceptions gently, and invite another attempt if useful.
Do not grade her or demand technical terminology when a clear plain-language explanation is sufficient.
CHALLENGE IT
Provide one short, meaningful challenge.
Examples:
- Trace blood from the heart to the lungs and back.
- Explain why the body changes blood flow to the skin in cold conditions.
- Distinguish a peptide from a receptor.
- Explain why bone needs both an organic framework and mineral.
- Identify the difference between innate and adaptive immunity.
- Trace a medicine from absorption through elimination.
- Explain how a change in cellular growth regulation could contribute to cancer.
Challenges should test understanding rather than memorization.
REFLECT
Ask one concise question about what became clearer, what remains uncertain, or how the learner's understanding changed.
Use the existing Heartwood reflection and progress systems.
8. SCIENTIFIC ACCURACY AND HEALTH SAFETY
This is an educational anatomy and physiology course, not a diagnostic or treatment service.
Scientific accuracy is essential, particularly when discussing peptides, medicines, cancer, and other health-related subjects.
Apply these principles throughout:
- Distinguish established evidence from hypotheses and emerging research.
- Avoid claims that a biological mechanism proves a treatment works.
- Do not imply that all peptides have the same effects.
- Do not imply that all supplements are safe because they occur naturally or resemble substances found in the body.
- Do not provide personalized diagnoses, prescriptions, treatment plans, or experimental dosing instructions.
- Do not instruct the learner to begin, stop, or change a medicine without consulting an appropriate healthcare professional.
- Do not portray a single food, supplement, hormone, or peptide as a universal solution.
- Explain uncertainty and individual variation honestly.
- Use reputable medical and scientific references when the application supports citations.
- When discussing current approval status, clinical recommendations, or recent research, verify the information against up-to-date authoritative sources rather than relying on memory alone.
Prefer sources such as government health agencies, established medical institutions, peer-reviewed research, and systematic reviews. Clearly distinguish the strength and limitations of evidence from different types of studies.
For the peptide lessons, make a clear distinction among a substance's proposed mechanism, laboratory findings, animal studies, human clinical evidence, regulatory status, product quality, and known risks.
For cancer, avoid simplistic explanations that blame the disease on one cause or suggest that a particular supplement can prevent or cure it.
For cold hands and feet, explain the physiology and possible contributing factors without attempting to diagnose a person.
Use a calm, respectful tone. The aim is to improve understanding and help the learner ask better questions of healthcare professionals.

11. THE HEARTWOOD STANDARD
This course should leave the learner seeing the human body differently.
A cell should no longer be an abstract shape in a textbook.
Mitochondria should no longer be just a memorized phrase about the powerhouse of the cell.
Bones should no longer be thought of as lifeless supports.
Hormones and peptides should no longer be mysterious substances advertised as promising treatments.
The heart, blood vessels, lungs, brain, muscles, bones, immune system, reproductive organs, and other structures should begin to make sense as parts of one living, interconnected system.
The learner should become comfortable asking:
- What is happening?
- Why does it happen?
- What causes the next step?
- How do the different parts interact?
- What evidence supports this explanation?
- What do we still not know?
The course must respect the learner's intelligence while never assuming prior scientific knowledge.
The Heartwood promise remains:
“I've always wondered about that.”
“Let's go to the heart of it.”
Begin by inspecting the existing Heartwood application. Then implement this course within its established architecture, preserving the learning experience that already works.
