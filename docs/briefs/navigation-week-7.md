# Navigation — Week 7, the optional Field Week

> Part of the Heartwood University handoff. Read `README.md` and `NAVIGATION.md` first. Week 7 uses the same engine, stages, faculty voices, completion rule (Feynman submitted, 15 words minimum), Across Cultures cards and exports as Lessons 1–30. Only the differences are listed here.

## Why it exists

The handbook's Part V, *Reading the Natural World* (Chapters 17–22), had no lessons behind it. The learner chose to keep the course at six weeks and add Week 7 as an **optional bonus**. The course is still "six weeks, 30 lessons", and finishing Lesson 30 still means the course is complete.

## How "optional" shows on screen

- **Course page:** Week 7 sits below Week 6 under the heading "FIELD WEEK · OPTIONAL", with one line beneath it: "Five extra lessons on reading the land itself. Take them any time, in any order, or not at all."
- **Progress:** the home page's 30-tick strip doesn't change. Week 7 gets its own 5-tick row underneath, labeled "Field Week". It is never counted in "N of 30 complete".
- **Course completion:** never waits on Week 7. After Lesson 30, the course reads "Complete", and Field Week shows on its own line, for example "Field Week · 2 of 5".
- **Order:** the five lessons can be taken in any order. Nothing in Week 7 is locked, and nothing elsewhere in the course depends on it.
- **Numbering:** Lessons 31–35. Store them under `heartwood.navigation.lesson31` to `lesson35`.

## The five lessons

Part V has six chapters. Chapters 19 and 20 (plants and animals) share Lesson 33.

| Lesson | Title | Feeds | Concept |
|---|---|---|---|
| 31 | The wind has a shape | Ch 17 Wind and Weather | Wind is a local fact bent by terrain, not a compass |
| 32 | Water knows the way down | Ch 18 Water and Drainage | Drainage describes the terrain; it doesn't give directions |
| 33 | Living signs | Ch 19 Plants, Ch 20 Animals | A clue to conditions isn't a clue to north |
| 34 | Reading rock and snow | Ch 21 Geological and Topographical Clues | Marks record local forces, which need regional context |
| 35 | The land people left | Ch 22 Human-Made and Cultural Landscape Clues | Human patterns follow rules, and the rules vary by place |

### Simulation ideas

These are starting points. As with every other lesson, the numbers must be real and the picture must never contradict its caption.

- **31 — Wind.** A ridge-and-valley map with a prevailing wind the learner can set. Local wind arrows bend along the valleys and around the ridge. A flagged tree at a chosen spot shows *its* wind, which differs from the prevailing one. Add a day/night toggle for an onshore and offshore breeze at a lake shore.
- **32 — Water.** A contour sheet with streams. Tapping any stream highlights its whole watershed, and V-shaped contours point upstream. One required case: a "follow it downstream" route that ends in a swamp or a gorge rather than a road.
- **33 — Living signs.** A small hill with north- and south-facing slopes, and vegetation that changes with aspect and moisture. The learner surveys moss on 40 trees in a shaded ravine and 40 on an open slope, and the tally shows where moss really grows. A second panel: game trails converging on water, not lining up with a direction.
- **34 — Rock and snow.** A regional map of ice flow with striations sampled at several sites, so the learner sees the direction change from lobe to lobe. Ohio's glacial history is a good fit. A second panel: cornices and drifts forming on the lee side of the *local* wind.
- **35 — The land people left.** Ohio is ideal. Compare a township in the Public Land Survey grid, where roads run on section lines (N–S and E–W), with the Virginia Military District, where metes-and-bounds surveys left roads that follow no grid. Grid roads make a useful compass; the irregular ones are a trap.

### Required cautions (from the handbook spec)

Each one must appear clearly in the lesson's text, not in a footnote:

- **31:** wind direction is not a universal compass.
- **32:** V-shaped contours generally point upstream. Not every junction or valley shape is a directional sign. **Safety:** following a stream downstream is not guaranteed to lead to people, roads or safety.
- **33:** "Moss always grows on the north side" is unreliable; explain why. Animal behavior is an environmental clue, not a dependable compass.
- **34:** glacial markings show local ice movement, not necessarily north–south, and reading them needs regional geological context.
- **35:** some bent trees are culturally recognized markers, but the broad claim that all or most unusually bent trees were made by Indigenous people as directional markers is **not established**. Label it honestly, using the evidence rules in `NAVIGATION.md`.

### Across Cultures

One card per lesson, under the same rules as before: evidence label, a "What remains uncertain" note, and real sources, or else no card. Suggested places to start research. These are not claims to print:

- **31:** Inuit wayfinding by snowdrifts and wind-shaped snow ridges (*sastrugi*); see Aporta's work on Igloolik.
- **32:** a culture whose routes or place names follow drainage systems.
- **35:** a documented road or trail network, such as the Inca *Qhapaq Ñan* or a recorded Indigenous trail system in the Ohio Valley.
