Heartwood Field Guide
Finding Your Way Without Modern Technology
Companion handbook development specification for Claude
1. Purpose
Create a comprehensive, illustrated, printable field handbook to accompany the Heartwood University course, "The Art and Science of Navigation."
The handbook should teach practical navigation using:
The Earth and its physical characteristics.
The Sun, Moon, and stars.
A magnetic compass.
Maps and terrain.
Natural observations.
Simple measuring instruments.
Traditional navigation knowledge from different cultures.
It should be useful to a beginner, but substantial enough that an experienced outdoorsperson can use it as a reference.
The handbook must be understandable without access to the online course.
2. Design and format
Create a polished, print-friendly handbook.
Use US Letter paper, portrait orientation.
Include a cover, table of contents, page numbers, section headings, diagrams, and an index.
Use readable typography and generous margins.
Include black-and-white-friendly illustrations so the handbook can be printed economically.
Use color where it genuinely improves understanding.
Provide printable field worksheets and blank recording tables.
Include a glossary, reference tables, and a bibliography.
Use consistent symbols for safety notes, practical exercises, historical notes, and cautions.
Every illustration should teach something. Avoid decorative images that do not contribute to understanding.
Where possible, create original diagrams rather than relying on copyrighted illustrations.

Chapter 5: The Shadow Stick Method
Teach two methods separately.
Method A: Approximate two-mark method.
Place a straight stick vertically in level ground.
Mark the shadow tip.
Wait approximately 15–20 minutes.
Mark the new shadow tip.
Draw a line between the marks.
Use the line as an approximate east-west reference, with the first mark generally toward the west and the second toward the east.
Draw a perpendicular line to estimate north-south.
Explain that this is an approximation. Accuracy varies with season, latitude, and observation time.
Method B: Equal-shadow method.
Place a vertical stick in level ground.
Draw a circle around its base.
Mark where the shadow tip first touches the circle.
Wait until the shadow tip reaches the circle again.
Connect the two marks.
The line approximates east-west.
Draw a perpendicular line to establish north-south.
Explain why this method is generally more dependable.
Include diagrams showing both methods and their limitations.
Chapter 6: Finding Solar Time
Difference between solar time and clock time.
Local solar noon.
Why the Sun is highest in the sky at local solar noon.
The equation of time, explained simply.
Time zones and longitude.
Estimating time using shadows.
Using a compass to establish direction and interpret the shadow.
Important: A compass alone cannot tell time. The Sun and shadow provide the time clue; the compass provides a directional reference.
Include a simple field exercise and a solar-time recording sheet.
Chapter 7: Navigating by the Stars
Celestial sphere.
Earth's rotation and apparent star movement.
Big Dipper pointer stars.
Polaris.
Cassiopeia.
Southern Cross.
Celestial poles.
Star altitude and latitude.
Why star paths depend on latitude and hemisphere.
Include diagrams of the northern and southern skies.
Explain that Polaris is close to, but not exactly at, the north celestial pole. Its altitude above the horizon provides an approximate latitude in the Northern Hemisphere.
Do not teach that the highest point of any star's path universally indicates south. Star paths depend on the star's declination, observer's latitude, and direction of observation.

Chapter 10: Taking and Following a Bearing
Hold the compass level.
Point the direction-of-travel arrow at the target.
Rotate the housing.
Align the magnetic needle.
Read the bearing.
Select a landmark along the bearing.
Walk to the landmark and repeat.
Explain common errors:
Holding the compass at an angle.
Standing near metal.
Confusing the magnetic needle with the direction-of-travel arrow.
Forgetting declination.
Following a bearing without checking terrain.
Include a practical exercise with several nearby landmarks.
Chapter 11: Using a Compass with a Map
Teach the complete process:
Place the map on a flat surface.
Align the compass with the map's north-south grid or meridian.
Rotate the map until the compass indicates the correct orientation, accounting for declination where required.
Identify landmarks.
Take a bearing to a visible object.
Transfer the bearing to the map.
Identify the object's approximate location.
Distinguish between:
Plotting the location of an object from a known position.
Finding your own position using bearings to known landmarks.
Chapter 12: Triangulation and Resection
Explain the terminology carefully.
Taking bearings to two known landmarks from an unknown position.
Drawing lines from those landmarks back toward the observer.
Finding the intersection as an estimate of the observer's position.
Using a third landmark to improve confidence.
Understanding error and why the lines may not meet at one exact point.
Include a complete illustrated example.

Chapter 14: Measuring Height with a Compass
Teach how to estimate the height of a tree, building, or other visible object using a compass with a clinometer.
Required equipment:
Compass with clinometer, or a simple homemade sighting device.
Measuring tape or known horizontal distance.
Recording sheet.
Procedure:
Stand at a measured horizontal distance from the base of the object.
Sight the top of the object.
Read the vertical angle.
Calculate height above eye level:
Height above eye level = horizontal distance × tangent of vertical angle.
Add the observer's eye height when appropriate.
Include a worked example using a 30-degree angle and a 20-foot horizontal distance.
Explain that sloping ground, inaccurate distance measurements, and poor sighting affect the result.

5. Evidence and accuracy standards
Use these labels throughout:
Well documented: Strong historical, scientific, or practical support.
Documented regionally: Reliable evidence exists, but the practice is not universal.
Plausible but uncertain: Some evidence or interpretation exists, but conclusions remain unsettled.
Modern interpretation: A present-day explanation or reconstruction rather than a clearly documented historical practice.
Distinguish scientific principles from cultural interpretations. Respect living cultural traditions and avoid claiming ownership or universal use of knowledge.

7. Relationship to the Heartwood course
Use the same terminology and lesson sequence as the online course.
Where appropriate, identify the corresponding course lesson at the beginning of each handbook chapter.
The handbook should provide additional depth, practical instructions, and reference material beyond the online lessons.
A student should be able to read the handbook independently, but a student taking the class should find the two resources reinforce each other.
8. Development and quality control
Before creating the handbook:
Review the completed Heartwood navigation course.
Confirm that the terminology and lesson references match.
Research historical and cultural claims.
Verify all formulas and worked examples.
Create diagrams that accurately represent the instructions.
Test every practical exercise for clarity and feasibility.
Check that each chapter can be understood independently.
Proofread for consistency and print readability.
Build the handbook in complete chapters, not as a collection of short notes.
Do not use placeholder text, fabricated references, or invented historical examples.
9. Final deliverable
Produce a comprehensive, illustrated, printable field handbook with:
A finished cover.
Table of contents.
Complete chapters.
Original diagrams.
Worked calculations.
Field exercises.
Worksheets.
Glossary.

Index.
The finished handbook should feel like a dependable reference book that a person would keep on a bookshelf, take on a camping trip, or pass along to someone who wants to understand how people find their way.
The guiding philosophy: Teach people to observe carefully, understand what they see, question what they assume, and combine evidence before deciding where to go.
