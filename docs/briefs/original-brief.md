HEARTWOOD UNIVERSITY
Physics I — Seeing the World Through Physics
Six weeks • 30 minutes a day • No prerequisites • Understanding over memorization
Our goal
At the end of six weeks, I don't want you to be able to pass a physics exam.
I want you to be able to stand beside a bridge, watch a car stop, throw a ball, hear an airplane overhead, boil water, or drop something—and have a little voice in your head say:
“I know what's happening here.”
That is our measure of success.

Our First Principle
Every Heartwood course should begin with a question rather than a definition.
So Physics doesn't begin with:
Physics is the study of matter, energy, motion and forces.
That's technically fine—and educationally rather dull.
Instead:
Why doesn't the world collapse?
A bridge holds thousands of pounds.
A chair holds you.
A ladder leans against a wall.
A building stands in the wind.
A baseball flies through the air.
A tiny magnet can pull a nail.
A glass sits on a table without falling through it.
Why?
Physics is our attempt to understand the rules underneath all of those things.
That's our doorway.

The world is moving—even when it looks still.
We'll explore:
- Motion
- Speed
- Acceleration
- Force
- Mass
- Gravity
- Inertia
- Momentum
Our everyday laboratory:
Cars, bicycles, balls, walking, elevators and falling objects.
Big question:
Why does a passenger lurch forward when the car suddenly stops?

WEEK 2 — Forces: The Push and Pull of the World
We'll discover:
- Push and pull
- Friction
- Tension
- Compression
- Gravity
- Balanced and unbalanced forces
- Why objects bend, break and support weight
And we'll return to your bridge.
Why can a bridge carry a truck that weighs many tons?
You'll begin seeing compression and tension in ordinary objects.
A chair becomes physics.
A ladder becomes physics.
Your knees become physics.

WEEK 3 — Energy: Nothing Comes for Free
This may be one of the most important ideas in the entire course.
We'll explore:
- Potential energy
- Kinetic energy
- Heat
- Work
- Conservation of energy
- Energy transformation
- Efficiency
We'll ask:
Where does the energy go when a moving car stops?
And:
How can a 10-pound weight falling a few feet eventually produce electricity?
By the end of this week, "energy" should stop being a vague word.
You'll begin recognizing energy changing forms everywhere.

WEEK 4 — Heat, Pressure and the Invisible World
Now we'll enter the world we can't see.
We'll explore:
- Molecules
- Temperature
- Heat
- Pressure
- Expansion
- Contraction
- Fluids
- Buoyancy
Questions like:
Why does a hot-air balloon rise?
Why does a tire get warmer while driving?
Why does a huge steel ship float?

Why can you drink through a straw?
Suddenly, everyday objects become demonstrations.

WEEK 5 — Waves, Sound and Light
We'll explore:
- Waves
- Frequency
- Wavelength
- Sound
- Resonance
- Light
- Reflection
- Refraction
- Color
And we'll ask:
What actually happens when you hear someone's voice?
Then:
What is happening inside your hearing aids?
A subject you've encountered personally becomes a physics laboratory.

WEEK 6 — Physics Is Everywhere
This week is different.
Instead of learning new material, you become the physicist.
We'll revisit:
- Motion
- Forces
- Energy
- Pressure
- Heat
- Waves
- Light
But now you explain them.
I'll give you things from ordinary life:
A bridge.A refrigerator.A car.A bicycle.A ceiling fan.A microwave.A boat.An airplane.A basketball.A flashlight.
And you'll tell me what's happening.
Not with equations.
With understanding.

And here's where our experiment gets interesting
I think every Heartwood lesson should have five stages.
1. SEE IT
Something real.
Here's a bridge.
2. UNDERSTAND IT
I'll explain the smallest amount necessary to give you the mental model.
3. CONNECT IT
Where else have you seen this?
4. FEYNMAN IT
You explain it back to me using ordinary language and an everyday comparison.
5. CHALLENGE IT
The Editor asks:
"Are you sure?"
And pushes your understanding a little further.

And there will be no shame in saying:
“I don't get it.”
That may actually become one of the most important sentences at Heartwood.
When you say it, I don't simply repeat the explanation.
The Roommate changes direction.
Maybe we use a car.
Maybe a bucket of water.
Maybe a chair.
Maybe we draw it.
Maybe we tell a story.
Maybe we build something.
We keep looking for the doorway into your understanding.

One more rule I'd like to establish
Heartwood University does not rush.
If something important takes two days instead of one, we take two days.
If something is difficult, we don't hide it behind fancy words.
And if you understand something beautifully but technically incorrectly, the Editor catches it.
Our objective isn't:
“I finished six weeks.”
It is:
“I understand physics better than I did six weeks ago.”

Heartwood University — Claude Code Build Specification
HEARTWOOD UNIVERSITY
Claude Code Build Specification — Prototype Version 1.0
Project Purpose
Build the first prototype of Heartwood University, a personal learning environment designed to teach broad understanding of important subjects.
Heartwood is not intended to reproduce a traditional college education.
Its purpose is:
Go to the heart of a subject so the student can see it, understand it, explain it, and recognize it in everyday life.
The first course will be:
Physics I — Seeing the World Through Physics
This course is the prototype for the entire Heartwood educational system.
Do not build the entire university yet.
Build the Physics course well enough that a real student can use it for six weeks, then use what we learn to improve the Heartwood learning model.

1. EDUCATIONAL PHILOSOPHY
Heartwood University is based on several principles.
Understanding over memorization
The student should understand how something works rather than memorize terminology.
Mental pictures
Whenever possible, concepts should be represented mentally through:
- everyday objects
- simple diagrams
- demonstrations
- stories
- comparisons
- physical experiences
Everyday application
Every important concept should answer:
"Where do I see this in my life?"
Feynman method
The student should periodically explain an idea in simple language.
A core test is:
"Can I explain this to an intelligent 12-year-old without hiding behind technical vocabulary?"
Curiosity
Lessons should begin with interesting questions rather than textbook definitions.
Instead of:
"Today we will study Newton's First Law."
Start with:
"Why does your body move forward when a car suddenly stops?"
No shame in confusion
The student should be encouraged to say:
"I don't understand."
When that happens, the system should explain the idea in a different way rather than simply repeating the same explanation.
Challenge understanding
The system should occasionally challenge the student's explanation.
The goal is to detect false understanding.

2. THE HEARTWOOD FACULTY
The prototype should support four teaching roles.
THE PROFESSOR
Purpose:
Explain the concept clearly.
Characteristics:
- knowledgeable
- patient
- visual
- concise
- avoids unnecessary academic language
- introduces terminology only when useful
The Professor answers:
"How does this work?"

THE ROOMMATE
Purpose:
Explore the idea conversationally.
The Roommate should feel like an intelligent friend sitting at the kitchen table.
The student can say:
"I still don't get it."
The Roommate tries another approach.
Examples:
- analogy
- story
- physical object
- thought experiment
- simple drawing
- different example
- question
The Roommate answers:
"Let's figure this out together."

THE EDITOR
Purpose:
Test and challenge understanding.
The Editor should respectfully question the student's reasoning.
Examples:
"You are close, but I think you've mixed up mass and weight."
"Can you give me another example?"
"What would happen if we doubled the weight?"
"Does your explanation still work in this situation?"
The Editor answers:
"Do you really understand this?"
The Editor must never be condescending.

THE BUILDER
Purpose:
Connect knowledge to doing.
The Builder asks:
"Can we demonstrate this?"
Where practical, suggest:
- household demonstrations
- simple experiments
- drawings
- construction
- measurements
- observations
- real-world projects
The Builder answers:
"Let's see it happen."

3. DAILY LESSON LENGTH
Target:
30 minutes
A typical lesson consists of:
1. SEE IT — 5 minutes
Begin with an interesting real-world situation.
Example:
A large bridge carries thousands of tons.
Why doesn't it collapse?
2. UNDERSTAND IT — 8 minutes
Explain the minimum essential concepts needed to understand the phenomenon.
Avoid information overload.
3. CONNECT IT — 5 minutes
Ask where the student sees the principle in ordinary life.
4. FEYNMAN IT — 5 minutes
Student explains the idea in their own words.
Require an everyday comparison when appropriate.
Example:
"Explain force by comparing it to something you already understand."
5. CHALLENGE IT — 5 minutes
The Editor tests the student's understanding.
6. REFLECT — 2 minutes
Ask:
"What is one thing you understand now that you didn't understand before?"

4. SIX-WEEK PHYSICS COURSE
WEEK 1 — THE RULES OF MOTION
Central idea:
The world is moving—even when it appears still.
Topics:
- motion
- position
- speed
- acceleration
- force
- mass
- gravity
- inertia
- momentum
Real-world questions:
- Why does a passenger lurch forward when a car stops?
- Why does a bicycle stay upright while moving?
- Why does a ball eventually stop rolling?
- Why does a heavy object feel harder to accelerate?

WEEK 2 — FORCES
Central idea:
Everything around us is being pushed, pulled, supported, compressed, stretched, or resisted.
Topics:
- force
- friction
- tension
- compression
- support forces
- gravity
- balanced forces
- unbalanced forces
- bending
- structural strength
Major real-world case study:
WHY DOES A BRIDGE WORK?
Use this as a recurring example.
Explore:
- weight
- compression
- tension
- bending
- load distribution
- supports
- foundations
The student should eventually be able to look at a bridge and identify the basic forces acting on it.

WEEK 3 — ENERGY
Central idea:
Energy changes form.
Topics:
- potential energy
- kinetic energy
- work
- heat
- conservation of energy
- energy transfer
- efficiency
Questions:
- Where does the energy go when a car stops?
- How does a roller coaster gain and lose energy?
- How does a falling object gain speed?
- How does a power plant produce electricity?
- Why can't a machine be 100% efficient?

WEEK 4 — HEAT, PRESSURE AND THE INVISIBLE WORLD
Central idea:
Things we cannot see can produce effects we can see.
Topics:
- atoms and molecules
- temperature
- heat
- pressure
- expansion
- contraction
- fluids
- buoyancy
Questions:
- Why does a hot-air balloon rise?
- Why does a tire get warmer while driving?
- Why does a ship float?
- Why does a straw work?
- Why does a pressure cooker cook faster?

WEEK 5 — WAVES, SOUND AND LIGHT
Central idea:
Energy and information can travel as waves.
Topics:
- waves
- frequency
- wavelength
- amplitude
- sound
- resonance
- light
- reflection
- refraction
- color
Questions:
- What actually happens when we hear a voice?
- Why does an echo happen?
- Why can a guitar string produce different notes?
- Why does a straw look bent in water?
- How do mirrors work?
Where appropriate, connect the subject to hearing, speakers, microphones, cameras, and communication technology.

WEEK 6 — SEEING PHYSICS EVERYWHERE
This week introduces little new material.
The student becomes the observer and teacher.
Present ordinary objects and situations:
- bridge
- automobile
- bicycle
- ladder
- refrigerator
- ceiling fan
- boat
- airplane
- flashlight
- basketball
- playground swing
- household tools
Ask:
"What physics do you see?"
Require the student to explain the situation using concepts from the course.
The final objective is not a test score.
It is:
Can the student now recognize physics happening in ordinary life?

5. THE HEARTWOOD LEARNING LOOP
Every major concept should move through this sequence:
SEE
What is happening?
↓
WONDER
Why is it happening?
↓
DISCOVER
What principle explains it?
↓
CONNECT
Where else does this happen?
↓
EXPLAIN
Can I explain it simply?
↓
CHALLENGE
Does my explanation still work?
↓
USE
Can I predict something or apply the idea?
This sequence should become the fundamental learning architecture of Heartwood University.

6. KNOWLEDGE LEVELS
Do not use traditional grades as the primary measure.
Track understanding through five levels:
1. ENCOUNTERED
"I have heard of this."
2. RECOGNIZE
"I recognize it when I see it."
3. UNDERSTAND
"I understand the basic mechanism."
4. EXPLAIN
"I can explain it simply."
5. USE
"I can use the idea to predict or solve something."
The system should allow concepts to move forward or backward.
A student who previously understood something but later struggles should be able to revisit it.

7. Feynman CHECK
At appropriate points, ask the student to explain a concept.
Example:
Concept: Inertia
Prompt:
Explain inertia without using the word "inertia."
Then:
Give me an everyday example.
Then:
Give me a second example.
Then:
What would happen if the situation changed?
The system should evaluate the response for:
- conceptual correctness
- simplicity
- completeness
- incorrect assumptions
- misuse of terminology
Do not require perfect academic wording.
We care about understanding.

8. THE "I DON'T GET IT" SYSTEM
This is critical.
If the student indicates confusion, do not simply repeat the previous explanation.
Instead, offer a different mental route.
Possible routes:
Analogy
"Think of it like..."
Physical object
"Imagine holding..."
Story
"Picture this situation..."
Visual
Show or describe a simple diagram.
Experiment
"Try this with a chair and a book."
Question
Ask a smaller question that leads toward the concept.
Reverse explanation
Start with the result and work backward.
The Roommate should decide which approach is most appropriate.

9. THE EDITOR SYSTEM
The Editor should look for:
- memorized definitions without understanding
- circular explanations
- contradictions
- misuse of terminology
- overly complicated explanations
- incorrect analogies
- failure to transfer the idea to another situation
Example:
Student:
"A heavy object falls faster because gravity is stronger on it."
Editor:
"Interesting. Let's test that idea. If we dropped a 10-pound ball and a 5-pound ball from the same height at the same time, what would you predict?"
The purpose is not to catch the student.
The purpose is to expose and repair misconceptions.

10. DAILY MEMORY
At the end of each lesson, save a very small number of items.
Do not turn Heartwood into an Anki-style memorization system.
Possible saved items:
- concept
- student's explanation
- student's analogy
- misconception
- confidence
- real-world connection
- question still unanswered
Example:
CONCEPT:
Force
STUDENT'S EXPLANATION:
"A force is a push or pull that can change motion."
EVERYDAY CONNECTION:
"Pushing a shopping cart."
UNDERSTANDING:
Strong
OPEN QUESTION:
"How can a force exist when something isn't moving?"
This information should help future lessons adapt to the student.

13. LESSON DESIGN REQUIREMENTS
Every lesson should contain:
- lesson title
- opening question
- real-world situation
- simple explanation
- mental model
- everyday analogy
- real-world applications
- Feynman challenge
- Editor challenge
- optional Builder activity
- reflection
- concept(s) saved to the Understanding Map
Avoid:
- long lectures
- unnecessary dates/history
- excessive formulas
- academic jargon
- multiple new concepts at once
- quizzes that test vocabulary rather than understanding
Equations can be introduced when they help explain something.
They should never be introduced simply because physics normally contains equations.

14. FIRST LESSON
Build the first lesson around:
WHY DOES A CAR'S PASSENGER MOVE FORWARD WHEN THE CAR STOPS?
Opening:
You're sitting in a car.
The car is traveling 40 mph.
The driver suddenly hits the brakes.
The car stops.
Your body moves forward.
Why?
Do not immediately give the answer.
Let the student think.
Then gradually introduce:
- motion
- inertia
- force
- acceleration/deceleration
- relationship between the car and the passenger
Use everyday comparisons.
Example:
Imagine standing in the back of a pickup truck.
The truck accelerates.
What happens to you?
Then:
What happens when it stops?
The student should arrive at the idea before receiving the formal terminology.
At the end:
Feynman Challenge
Explain why you move forward when a car suddenly stops without using the word "inertia."
Everyday Comparison
Compare inertia to something you have experienced in ordinary life.
Editor Challenge
If inertia is not a force, why does it feel like something is pushing you forward?
This is the type of thinking Heartwood should produce.
