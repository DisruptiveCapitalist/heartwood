LESSON 03
Why a rolling ball eventually stops
Concept: Friction
Opening question:
You roll a ball across the floor. Nobody touches it. Nothing appears to stop it. Yet it eventually stops. Where did the motion go?
SEE IT
Create a simple rolling-ball simulation.
Allow the learner to change:
- surface
- initial speed
- ball mass
Use surfaces such as:
- ice
- wood
- carpet
- grass
The important visual observation:
The ball does not simply "decide" to stop.
The surface interacts with the ball continuously.
Professor prompt:
Don't worry about the word friction yet. Watch what happens.
Ask:
Which ball travels farthest?
Then:
What is different about the surfaces?
UNDERSTAND IT
Friction is an interaction between surfaces that resists relative motion.
A rolling ball slows because energy is being transferred through:
- deformation of the ball and surface
- microscopic interactions
- sound
- heat
Important correction:
Friction does not mean "something wants to stop."
It means surfaces interacting oppose relative sliding or rolling motion.
Connect immediately to Lesson 1:
If inertia means an object keeps moving unless something changes its motion, then friction is one of the things that can change it.
CONNECT IT
Ask the learner to notice:
- shoes gripping the floor
- tires gripping the road
- a bicycle brake
- rubbing hands together
- sliding furniture
- ice skating
FEYNMAN IT
Explain to a 10-year-old why a ball stops rolling without saying "because it loses momentum."
CHALLENGE IT
Editor question:
If friction always opposes motion, why can you walk forward?
Expected reasoning:
Your foot pushes backward against the ground. Static friction from the ground pushes your foot forward.
REFLECT
Today I noticed friction when...
Understanding Map: Friction, Force, Motion, Energy, Inertia

LESSON 04
Why a heavy thing is harder to get moving
Concept: Mass and inertia
Opening question:
Why can you shove an empty shopping cart easily, but the same cart loaded with 200 pounds feels completely different?
SEE IT
Create a push simulation with two objects of different mass.
Apply the same push.
Show:
- light object accelerates more
- heavy object accelerates less
Then allow the learner to change the applied force.
UNDERSTAND IT
Mass is a measure of how resistant an object is to changes in motion.
This is inertia expressed quantitatively.
The key relationship:
More mass → more resistance to acceleration.
Do not begin with equations.
First build the physical intuition.
Then introduce:
F = ma
as a compact description of what the learner has just seen.
CONNECT IT
Compare:
- empty vs full wheelbarrow
- empty vs loaded backpack
- bicycle vs car
- baseball vs bowling ball
- pushing a door vs pushing a refrigerator
FEYNMAN IT
Why doesn't pushing twice as hard make a heavy object magically behave like a light one?
CHALLENGE IT
Does a heavy object always fall faster?
Use this to separate mass from gravity.
REFLECT
Mass is not simply "how heavy something is." It is also...
Understanding Map: Mass, Inertia, Force, Acceleration

LESSON 05
What momentum actually measures
Concept: Momentum
Opening question:
Which is harder to stop: a bicycle moving slowly or a truck moving slowly? Why?
SEE IT
Create a collision/stop simulation.
Allow changes in:
- mass
- velocity
Show that both matter.
Introduce:
momentum = mass × velocity
UNDERSTAND IT
Momentum describes how much motion an object carries in a way that matters when objects interact.
A large mass moving slowly can have substantial momentum.
A small mass moving very fast can also have substantial momentum.
Do not portray momentum as a mysterious substance inside an object.
It is a quantity we use to describe motion.
CONNECT IT
Examples:
- baseball
- golf ball
- bowling ball
- car
- truck
- hammer
- football
FEYNMAN IT
Explain why catching a fast baseball with soft hands hurts less than stopping it suddenly with a rigid hand.
CHALLENGE IT
Ask:
If a moving object has momentum, where does that momentum go when it stops?
Connect to forces, the surroundings, and conservation of momentum.
REFLECT
I used to think momentum was ______. Now I think it is ______.
Understanding Map: Momentum, Mass, Velocity, Force, Inertia

IMPLEMENTATION RULES FOR ALL LESSONS 3–30
1. Preserve the six-stage architecture
Every lesson must contain:
- SEE IT
- UNDERSTAND IT
- CONNECT IT
- FEYNMAN IT
- CHALLENGE IT
- REFLECT
Do not replace these with a conventional textbook structure.

2. Preserve the Heartwood voices
THE PROFESSOR
Clear, calm, concise.
The Professor introduces the idea but does not lecture endlessly.
THE ROOMMATE
The learner's conversational partner.
If the learner says:
"I don't get it."
provide the existing routes:
- Give me an analogy
- Use something in my hand
- Tell me a story
- Let's try it right now
- Ask me a smaller question
- Start from the end
Do not simply repeat the original explanation.
THE EDITOR
The Editor is not a quizmaster.
The Editor looks for:
- memorized definitions
- circular explanations
- contradictions
- terminology used without understanding
- bad analogies
- explanations that are too complicated
- failure to transfer the concept
The Editor should sometimes say:
"That sounds right, but I don't think you've explained why."
or:
"You used the word, but I'm not sure you understand the idea behind it."
THE BUILDER
Whenever practical, give the learner something physical to do.
The Builder should prefer:
- household objects
- hands
- walking
- a ball
- a chair
- a book
- a rubber band
- a glass of water
- a flashlight
over elaborate equipment.

3. DO NOT OVERUSE EQUATIONS
Equations should appear only after the physical idea is understood.
The sequence should generally be:
experience → observation → idea → relationship → equation
not:
equation → definition → memorize

4. USE SIMULATIONS WHEN THEY ACTUALLY HELP
Interactive demonstrations should allow the learner to change something and observe what happens.
Good:
- change mass
- change force
- change friction
- change speed
- move a load
- change pressure
- change temperature
- change frequency
- change angle
Avoid simulations that are merely animated illustrations.
The learner should have something to notice.

5. KEEP THE "I DON'T GET IT" SYSTEM
Every lesson should have an easy way to stop and say:
I don't get it
The system should route the learner to another representation rather than simply supplying more text.
Possible transformations:
Analogy → physical object → story → experiment → smaller question → reverse explanation

7. DAILY MEMORY
Do not convert Physics I into an Anki-style memorization system.
For each completed lesson, preserve the learner's:
- concept
- own explanation
- analogy
- misconception
- confidence
- real-world connection
- open question
This creates a personal history of understanding.

8. JOURNAL
Continue the existing reflective style.
Useful prompts include:
Today I finally understood...
I see this differently now...
I noticed this in real life...
I still don't understand...
Something I want to explore...
Do not require long writing.
A few sentences are enough.

9. LESSON COMPLETION
Completion should require meaningful participation, not perfect answers.
A learner should be able to complete a lesson by:
- seeing the demonstration
- engaging with the explanation
- making a connection
- attempting the Feynman explanation
- responding to the challenge
- reflecting
There are no grades.

10. PROGRESSION
The course should feel like a spiral.
Later lessons should deliberately reactivate earlier ideas.
Examples:
Lesson 3 → friction builds on inertia.
Lesson 4 → mass deepens inertia.
Lesson 5 → momentum combines mass and motion.
Lesson 9 → forces become structural.
Lesson 10 → force becomes torque.
Lesson 11 → motion becomes energy.
Lesson 14 → energy becomes electricity.
Lesson 18 → forces explain floating.
Lesson 23 → waves and electricity become personal.
Lessons 26–30 → everything comes back together.

11. DO NOT MAKE THE FINAL WEEK FEEL LIKE A TEST
Week 6 is the learner becoming independent.
The Professor should gradually step aside.
The learner should increasingly say:
"I think I know what's happening here."
That is the point.

HEARTWOOD PHYSICS I COMPLETION STANDARD
At the end of Lesson 30, the learner does NOT need to remember every term.
Instead, Heartwood should ask:
CAN YOU SEE IT?
Can you notice the physical phenomenon?
CAN YOU EXPLAIN IT?
Can you explain it in ordinary language?
CAN YOU CONNECT IT?
Can you connect it to something familiar?
CAN YOU USE IT?
Can you use the idea to explain a new situation?
CAN YOU RECOGNIZE IT LATER?
Can you encounter the phenomenon in ordinary life and realize:
"I know something about what's happening here."
That is successful completion of Physics I.
