**The numbers behind it.** Errors against true east–west at Columbus, with a 20-minute wait, by when the first mark is made: on 20 March 0.1° at any hour, because the shadow tip runs dead straight that day; on 1 May 0.8° at noon and 14.9° four hours before; on 21 June 1.0° and 21.8°; on 21 December 2.2° and 30.7°. The afternoon is consistently a shade worse than the morning — 23.3° four hours AFTER noon in June against 21.8° four hours before — so a table of these figures has to say which side of noon it means. The equal-shadow method on the same days: 0.09° at the equinox, 0.07° on 1 May, 0.00° at both solstices. The two fail under opposite conditions — the two-mark method is beaten by the curvature of the tip's path, which is nil at the equinoxes and greatest at the solstices, while the equal-shadow method is beaten only by the Sun's declination drifting during the day, which is fastest at the equinoxes and nil at the solstices. Lengthening the wait from 15 to 20 minutes moves the error by about two tenths of a degree: the hour of the day matters, the wait barely does.

> _For the handbook:_ Chapter 5 asks for the two methods to be taught separately and the first to be labelled approximate. It should go further and say approximate BY HOW MUCH, with a figure and a date — "about 21° wrong at Columbus two hours before noon in December" is a sentence a reader can act on, and "approximate" is not. The chapter's instruction to wait 15 to 20 minutes should carry a note that the wait is not the sensitive quantity; working within an hour of midday is worth ten times more than any wait. Both diagrams should be drawn from the same first mark, as the simulation does, so the comparison is visibly fair.

> **Field Guide — Chapter 5: The Shadow Stick Method.** Do both methods on the same afternoon, from the same stick, and draw both lines. Then wait for a clear night and check them against Polaris. The gap between your two lines is not a mistake you made — it is the thing this lesson is about, and it will be widest in December.

**What the learner should notice**
- On 21 December, with the first mark two hours before noon, the two-mark line is **21.1° off** true east–west. Four hours before noon it is **30.7°** off. That is not a rough bearing, it is the wrong way.
- The equal-shadow line, drawn from the same first mark, is **0.00°** off on the same day. The difference between the two methods is not small and it is not a matter of care.
- Now switch to 20 March. The two-mark line is **0.1°** off — better than the careful method's 0.09°, near enough a tie. The crude method is not bad. It is bad on most days.

## UNDERSTAND IT
*The Professor:* Three sentences, and the third is the one that will keep you found.

**The three sentences**
1. The two-mark method draws a **chord across a curve**. Yesterday you watched the shadow tip trace a curve that bends away from the stick; a straight line between two points twenty minutes apart is a chord of it, and a chord only runs east–west where the curve itself does. At noon it nearly does. Four hours out it does not come close.
2. The equal-shadow method is built on **symmetry instead of straightness**. Two moments when the shadow is the same length sit at equal angles either side of the meridian, so the line joining them is square to the meridian — which is to say, east–west — and its accuracy does not depend on how curved the path is at all.
3. Each fails in the other's good conditions. The curve is **straight at the equinoxes and most bent at the solstices**, so the crude method is perfect in March and hopeless in December. The symmetry is broken only by the Sun's declination **drifting during the day**, which is fastest at the equinoxes and nil at the solstices. There is no day of the year on which both are bad.

### TWO WAYS TO FIND THE MIDDLE OF A ROOM
You want the centre line of a room. Method one: stand near one wall, take two steps along it, and sight a line through those two footprints. If the wall is straight you are right; if the wall curves even slightly, you are projecting the error the whole length of the room. Method two: find two points, one on each side, that are the same distance from the far corner, and join them. You do not need to know anything about the walls. The second method is uglier, needs more walking, and is right. That is the equal-shadow method's entire argument.

The Field Guide says to wait fifteen to twenty minutes between the marks, which makes it sound as though the wait is what you are getting wrong. It is not. At Columbus on 21 June, two hours before noon, waiting fifteen minutes gives **11.4°** of error and waiting twenty gives **11.2°**. The wait barely matters. **When you start** changes the answer by a factor of thirty, and almost nobody says so.

### The formal names
| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Two-mark method** | Mark the shadow tip, wait, mark it again, call that line east–west. Fast, needs no patience, and approximate in a way that depends violently on the date and the hour. |
| **Equal-shadow method** | Mark the tip in the morning, draw a circle through it centred on the stick, and mark where the tip returns to the circle in the afternoon. The line joining the two marks is east–west. |
| **Chord** | A straight line joining two points on a curve. The two-mark method's line is a chord, which is the whole of its problem. |
| **Hyperbola** | The shape the shadow tip traces on any day except the equinoxes. On the equinoxes the Sun's declination is zero and the shape degenerates into a straight line. |
| **Symmetry about the meridian** | The property the good method leans on: the Sun is as high an hour before noon as an hour after, so the shadow is as long before as after. |
| **Systematic error** | An error that is the same every time you repeat the measurement, so repeating it does not help. The two-mark method's error is systematic — taking ten careful readings on a December morning gives you ten wrong answers that agree. |
| **Perpendicular** | At right angles. Having found east–west, north–south is the perpendicular through the stick — and getting THAT square matters as much as the line itself. |
| **Declination drift** | The Sun's declination changing during the day — up to about 0.4° between dawn and dusk near an equinox. It is the only thing the equal-shadow method cannot defend itself against. |

## CONNECT IT
*The Roommate:* "Roughly right" is a thing people say about a lot of methods. Where have you met it?

**Where it shows up**
- A recipe that says "about 20 minutes" and burns at 18.
- Judging distance by eye and being badly wrong across water.
- A rule of thumb at work that everyone knows the exception to.
- Pacing out a room and finding the carpet doesn't fit.
- "Measure twice, cut once" — and still cutting it wrong.
- Weather folklore that works in one season and not another.

Every one of those is a method with a hidden condition attached — and the trouble is never that the method is bad, it is that the condition went unmentioned. The two-mark shadow method is a fine method in March. It is published without the month.

### The Builder — try it yourself
Try the crude method twice on the same day: once within half an hour of midday, once in the last hour before sunset. Mark both lines on the ground with string. They will not agree, and the evening one will be the wild one. You can do this in a car park. The disagreement between your two pieces of string is a measurement of the Earth's axial tilt, taken with stones.

### Across cultures — THE ISLAMIC WORLD, 9TH CENTURY ONWARDS
**Evidence label: WELL DOCUMENTED**

Muslims pray facing the Ka'ba in Mecca, which turns "which way is Mecca from here?" into a problem that had to be solved for every new town, in an era with no compass. Scholars attacked it from both ends. The mathematicians — al-Battani, al-Biruni and others — worked it as spherical trigonometry: given the latitude and longitude of both places, the direction of the great circle between them follows exactly, and al-Biruni's treatment of it is a serious piece of mathematics in its own right.

But there is also a shadow method, and it is the cleanest trick in this whole week. Twice a year the Sun passes directly over Mecca, and at those instants a vertical stick anywhere on the sunlit side of the Earth throws its shadow directly **away** from Mecca. You do not need to know your own latitude, your longitude, or any trigonometry at all — you need to know the date and the time, and then to look at the ground.

> **What remains uncertain:** How mosques were actually oriented is a separate question from how scholars said they should be, and the two often disagree: many early mosques face the direction of a tradition, a road grid, or the Prophet's own mosque rather than a computed great circle, and some are tens of degrees off a modern calculation. Whether particular early orientations were mistakes, deliberate alternative conventions, or built to a folk-astronomical scheme is still argued over.  
> **Sources:** King, "The Sacred Direction in Islam," *Interdisciplinary Science Reviews* (1985); King, *World-Maps for Finding the Direction and Distance to Mecca* (1999); al-Biruni, *Taḥdīd nihāyāt al-amākin*, translated by Ali as *The Determination of the Coordinates of Positions for the Correction of Distances between Cities* (1967).

_Do not restate this claim in the handbook without its label, its uncertainty and its sources._

## FEYNMAN IT
A friend has read the stick-and-two-stones method in a survival book and is about to use it to walk out of a wood in December, at four in the afternoon. Explain what will happen and what they should do instead.

Banned words: **approximate**, **roughly**, **accurate**

**A good explanation contains**
- It says the two-mark line is a chord of a curved path, and that is why it can be badly wrong.
- It gives a real number for how wrong — a figure from the simulation, not "quite a lot".
- It names the conditions that make it bad: far from noon, far from an equinox.
- It offers the equal-shadow method, or waiting for noon, as the thing to do instead.

## CHALLENGE IT
*The Editor:* One prediction and one question that has caught people out.

**You are going to use the two-mark method. Which change buys you the most accuracy?**

- _Doing it within half an hour of midday instead of late afternoon._  
  This one, by a mile. At Columbus in December that is the difference between about 2° and about 31°. Nothing else you can change comes close, and it costs nothing but waiting.

- _Waiting forty minutes between marks instead of fifteen._  
  Almost nothing. Measured at Columbus on 21 June, two hours before noon: 11.4° with a fifteen-minute wait, 11.2° with twenty. A longer wait gives you a longer line, which is easier to draw accurately, but it does not fix the error — the error is in the shape of the path, not in your draughtsmanship.

- _Using a taller stick so the marks are further apart._  
  A taller stick genuinely helps your DRAWING — Guo Shoujing's forty-*chi* gnomon in Lesson 12 was built on exactly that reasoning. But it scales the whole picture up, error included. A 21° error drawn at twice the size is still 21°.

**Both methods give you an east–west line. How would you get north–south from it, and where could that step go wrong?**

A perpendicular through the stick's base, and the honest way to build one is the old compass-and-string construction: from two points equally spaced along your east–west line, scribe equal arcs with a loop of string and join where they cross. Stepping it off by eye is where people quietly lose five degrees after doing the hard part correctly.

There is a subtler trap too. Your east–west line was drawn between two shadow-TIP marks, but the north–south line has to pass through the **base** of the stick, not the midpoint of the marks. On a day when the two marks are not symmetrical about the meridian — which is every day you use the crude method — the midpoint is not due east or west of the base, and taking the perpendicular at the midpoint instead of at the base adds a second error on top of the first.

## "I don't get it" — 7 ways in

### Explain it with a compass
Think of the two methods as two compasses. One of them has a needle that sticks: it reads correctly when you happen to be pointing one particular way and drifts further off the further you get from it. The other reads correctly everywhere but takes four hours. You would not say the sticky one is "approximate" — you would say it works at one bearing and lies at the others, and you would want to know which bearing. The two-mark method's good bearing is noon.

### Explain it with a map
Picture the shadow tip's path drawn on a map as a shallow curve running roughly east–west. The two-mark method takes a ruler, lays it across two points of that curve twenty minutes apart, and declares the ruler's direction to be east–west. Whether that is true depends entirely on which part of the curve the ruler is on — near the curve's lowest point it is nearly right, out on the arms it is tilted. The equal-shadow method never uses a ruler on the curve at all; it uses a pair of compasses centred on the stick.

### Use an everyday object
Take a dinner plate and lay a ruler across it near the edge, touching the rim at two points a short way apart. The ruler's direction depends on where on the rim you are — that is the two-mark method, and the plate's curve is the shadow path. Now instead tie a string to the plate's centre and find two points on the rim that the string reaches equally: the line between those two is square to the line from the centre, wherever you choose them. That is the equal-shadow method, and it does not care about the curve.

### Show me a diagram
In the simulation there are three marks, and the important one is the plain white circle: both methods start from exactly that mark. All that differs is what you do next. The red dashed line runs to a mark twenty minutes later. The green line runs to the mark where the shadow came back to the same LENGTH, which is the dotted circle round the stick. Watch the dotted circle as you drag the first mark: it is the whole method, drawn.

### Walk me through an example
Columbus, 21 December, first mark at 10:30 in the morning. Twenty minutes later you mark again and join them: your line is 21° off true east–west. You walk a mile on the north–south you build from it, and at 31 yards of error per mile per degree you finish about 680 yards from where you meant to be — well over a third of a mile, across a valley you did not intend to cross. Using the same first mark but waiting until the shadow is that length again — two hours the other side of noon, about half past two — your line is right to a hundredth of a degree, and the error after that mile is under a yard.

### Break it into smaller steps
Ask three questions in order. One: what shape does the shadow tip trace today? A straight line at the equinox, a curve otherwise. Two: does my method assume it is straight? The two-mark method does; the equal-shadow method does not. Three: how curved is it right now, where I am standing? Most curved far from noon and near the solstices. Put those together and you can predict, before you start, whether the quick method is safe today — which is the only skill in this lesson.

### Work backward from the answer
Start from the result: in December the crude method is 21° wrong two hours before noon, and the careful one is 0.00° wrong. Why would a method be exactly right? Because it rests on a symmetry rather than on an approximation — and symmetries are either there or not. Why would the other be 21° wrong rather than 2° or 40°? Because its error is the curvature of the path across a twenty-minute chord, and in December at 40° north, that is what the curvature comes to. Both answers come from the same picture: one method reads the curve, the other refuses to.
