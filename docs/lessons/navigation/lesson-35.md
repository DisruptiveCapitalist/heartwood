## SEE IT
*The Professor:* The same six miles of Ohio, surveyed two ways. Start on the grid and read the first two numbers.

**On-screen simulation:** `field` ({"mode":"survey"})

**What it shows.** The same six miles of country drawn two ways. **On the survey grid**, a township of thirty-six square-mile sections with a road on every line, the sections numbered back and forth as a real plat is — 6 to 1 along the top row right to left, 7 to 12 the other way, down to 36 in the south-east corner. **In the Virginia Military District**, the same ground with boundaries that follow a watercourse and each other, on no consistent bearing at all. A true north arrow in the corner, and a caption under each sheet saying what its lines are aligned to.

**What the learner can change.** Which system you are standing in; how good your compass is, from 1° to 6°; and how far this particular survey drifted from the plan, from 0° to 1.5°.

**The numbers behind it.** BY DESIGN the only thing pulling a Public Land Survey township off true north is the convergence of the meridians, which at 40° of latitude across six miles is 4.4 minutes of arc — 0.073°, or 2.2 yards in a mile, against a compass's 61.5 at two degrees, which is 27 times better. That is the plan and not the ground: the lines were run with a compass and a chain, Ohio's early surveys wander by a degree or more in places, and the Connecticut Western Reserve is on five-mile townships with its own scheme. A survey half a degree out is worth 17.6 yards a mile, still three and a half times a compass; one a degree and a half out has lost the advantage. A boundary run by metes and bounds sits on average 22° from the nearest cardinal, and nothing says which cardinal it was aiming at, so even that is the best of four guesses.

The wording to use is "designed to run 000°, 090°, 180° or 270°; on the ground, check one against your compass first". ## UNDERSTAND IT
*The Professor:* Three sentences to close the class, and the first one is the only good news in Field Week.

**The three sentences**
1. **A human mark is the only sign in this week that might have been aligned on purpose, and where it was, it is superb.** Everything else here records a force — wind, water, shade, ice — and a force has no interest in the pole. A surveyor does. When the United States laid out the Public Land Survey it SPECIFIED lines on true north, with correction lines at intervals for the convergence of the meridians, and the roads and field boundaries that followed those lines inherited whatever accuracy the surveyors achieved. On the plan that is a few minutes of arc. On the ground it is whatever a man with a compass and a chain managed, which in early Ohio is sometimes a degree or more — so the right posture is to check one road, once, against a bearing you trust. What survives the checking is the valuable part: a direction that is free, continuous for hundreds of miles, needs no declination, does not drift, and is visible in fog, at night and on a phone with a dead battery.
2. **But it is a fact about a system, and systems have edges.** The grid covers most of the United States west and north of the original colonies and stops dead where other arrangements were already in place — Spanish and French land grants, the Connecticut Western Reserve with its own scheme, and in Ohio the Virginia Military District, where warrants were taken up by metes and bounds in the Virginia manner: so many poles from the forked sycamore, along the creek to the stone. Those lines follow the ground, which means they follow drainage, which means they inherit everything Lesson 32 said about drainage. The roads that grew on them tell you about the landscape and nothing about north.
3. And **a human mark is evidence about people, which is why it has to be read with more care than a rock.** A road's bearing is a decision somebody made, and decisions have reasons: a surveyor's instruction, a river crossing, an older trail, a boundary agreed in a treaty, a landowner's refusal. That makes human signs the richest ones in the landscape and the easiest to over-read, because it is very tempting to supply a reason yourself. The discipline is the one from Lesson 30: ask what would have to be true for your reading to be right, and ask whether anything else would produce the same mark.

### THE LINES ON A TENNIS COURT AND THE PATH ACROSS THE PARK
A tennis court's lines are exactly where the rules say, because somebody measured them; if you want to know how long twenty-three metres is, go and look at one. The worn path across the park beside it is also a line, and also perfectly real, but it is where it is because that is the shortest way to the gate and the ground is dry there — nobody measured anything. Both are human marks on the ground. One carries a specification and the other carries a preference, and reading the second one as though it carried a specification is how people get lost in old country.

The American grid is the **Public Land Survey System**: **townships** six miles square, each divided into thirty-six **sections** of one square mile, with **correction lines** at intervals because converging meridians cannot tile a sphere. The older method it replaced is **metes and bounds**, which describes a boundary as a sequence of directions, distances and landmarks, and is how the **Virginia Military District** in Ohio was laid out — the one part of the state never put on a rectangular system. A road that follows a section line is a **section road**; the cardinal grid of such roads is the most reliable directional feature in American country. One caution belongs in plain sight rather than in a footnote. **Bent marker trees**: some trees deliberately shaped as markers are recognised by tribal historic preservation offices and by local tradition, and that is a real practice. The broad claim often made — that unusually bent or L-shaped trees are generally Indigenous trail markers — **is not established**, and most such trees have ordinary explanations: damage, snow load, competition, a fallen neighbour, a browsing animal. Under this course's evidence rules that claim is plausible and unproven, and it should be labelled that way wherever it is repeated.

### The formal names
| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Public Land Survey System** | The American rectangular survey. Townships of 36 square-mile sections, run on true north with periodic corrections. The reason so much of the country is a grid. |
| **Section line** | A boundary of one square mile. A road on one is MEANT to run a cardinal direction; how close it comes depends on who ran the survey and when. |
| **Section numbering** | Back and forth like a plough: 6 to 1 along the top row right to left, 7 to 12 the other way, and so on to 36 in the south-east corner. Section 1 is always the north-east corner. |
| **The Western Reserve** | Connecticut's claim in north-east Ohio, surveyed on FIVE-mile townships with its own numbering. One of several reasons "the grid" is not one thing even inside one state. |
| **Convergence** | Meridians closing towards the pole, which is why a rectangular survey needs correction lines and why the grid is not perfect. At Ohio's latitude, 4.4 minutes across a township. |
| **Metes and bounds** | A boundary described by bearings, distances and landmarks — "so many poles to the forked sycamore". Follows the ground, so it follows drainage. |
| **Virginia Military District** | The part of Ohio between the Little Miami and the Scioto, taken up on Virginia bounty warrants by metes and bounds. The only part of the state not on a rectangular system. |
| **Marker tree** | A tree deliberately shaped as a sign. Some are culturally recognised; the general claim about bent trees is not established, and most bending has ordinary causes. |

## CONNECT IT
*The Roommate:* Last one of the course. Where else can you tell something about the people who made a thing from the shape of what they left?

**Where it shows up**
- A path worn diagonally across a lawn, ignoring the paving.
- House numbers that jump, because a terrace was demolished.
- A road that bends round a field boundary that is no longer there.
- Street names that are all trees in one estate and all poets in the next.
- A wall with a straight bottom and a wavy top.
- The place in a car park everyone reverses into.

The road bending round a vanished field is the one that behaves like a section line, and it is the reason old country is so readable once you know what you are looking at. The bend is not a response to the terrain and not an accident; it is a fossilised decision, and the thing it respected has gone while the respect remains. That is exactly what a surveyed grid is too — a decision from 1785 that you can still walk along. The difference is only that one decision was "follow the boundary" and the other was "run due north", and you cannot tell which from standing on it.

### Across cultures — OHIO, BEFORE THE SURVEY
**Evidence label: DOCUMENTED REGIONALLY**

Before any of it was surveyed, Ohio had a road network, and much of it is still in use. William C. Mills's *Archaeological Atlas of Ohio* of 1914 mapped the trails as they were known then — among them the Great Trail across the north-east and the Scioto Trail running the length of the state from Sandusky Bay to the mouth of the Scioto — and concluded that those routes largely determined where the later improved highways and towns went. US Route 23 runs much of the Scioto Trail's line today.

What makes this the right card to end the course on is HOW those trails ran. The marker at Upper Arlington puts it plainly: the trails usually followed waterways. They were laid out by people solving the problem this week has been about — where is the ground passable, where is the water, where is the divide narrow — and the answer is a route that follows drainage. So Ohio's roads come in two kinds whose bearings mean two different things. The grid roads were run on true north by a surveyor with a compass and a chain, and they are an instrument. The trail roads follow the rivers, and reading one of them for direction is reading a watercourse, which is Lesson 32 all over again, with asphalt on it.

> **What remains uncertain:** Mills's atlas is over a century old, its trail positions are approximate — one modern compilation puts its accuracy at around seven kilometres — and no modern archaeological survey has systematically tested it. The language in the sources is carefully hedged, and should stay hedged: trails "usually" followed waterways and "often" determined later roads, which is not the same as always, and at least one documented trail never became a road at all. Claims about specific routes vary between sources, and the popular version — that a given modern highway "is" an Indigenous trail — compresses several centuries of re-routing into a single sentence.  
> **Sources:** Mills, *Archaeological Atlas of Ohio* (1914); Tanner (ed.), *Atlas of Great Lakes Indian History* (1987).

_Do not restate this claim in the handbook without its label, its uncertainty and its sources._

## CHALLENGE IT
*The Editor:* The last question of the course, and it is about a claim you will meet.

**Walking in old woodland you find a mature tree with a pronounced right-angle bend low on the trunk, growing horizontally for a metre and then turning up. Somebody tells you it is an Indigenous trail marker tree pointing the way to a ford. What should you do with that?**

- _Record it, and treat the claim as plausible and unproven — most bent trees have ordinary causes._  
  Right, and the balance here matters more than the verdict. Deliberately shaped marker trees are a real practice: some are recognised by tribal historic preservation offices and by documented local tradition, and dismissing the whole category would be both wrong and discourteous. But the broad claim — that unusually bent trees are generally Indigenous markers — is not established, and the ordinary explanations are abundant: a fallen neighbour pinning a sapling, snow load, browsing, storm damage, competition for light. Under this course's rules that is "plausible but uncertain", which is a label, not a dismissal. Record the tree, note the claim and its source, and do not navigate by it.

- _Take the bearing it points along — a marker tree is a human sign and this week said human signs are the reliable ones._  
  This takes the right lesson and applies it to the wrong case, which is the most interesting way to get it wrong. What made the section road reliable was not that a human made it; it was that a human made it to a WRITTEN SPECIFICATION, in a documented system, which is why we can say it is good to four minutes of arc. Nothing comparable exists here: no record of who shaped it, when, to what convention, or whether anybody shaped it at all. A human origin is not the thing that confers accuracy — a documented purpose is, and that is why the grid is in this lesson and the tree is in this question.

- _Dismiss it — trees bend for all sorts of reasons and the whole idea is folklore._  
  Too strong, and it fails the course's own standard in the opposite direction. The evidence rules this class has used all the way through have four labels and one of them is "plausible but uncertain", which exists precisely for claims like this: a real practice, genuine recognised examples, and a popular generalisation that outruns what can be shown. Replacing an overclaim with a dismissal is not scepticism, it is just a different unexamined confidence — and it would discard the documented cases along with the doubtful ones. The honest position is the one the label describes, and it takes no more effort to hold.

Every sign in Field Week is a true record of something, and every folk rule attached to it fails in the same way: it takes a reading about CONDITIONS and uses it as a reading about DIRECTION. Once you can hear that mistake you will hear it everywhere, including in things you have believed for years.

## "I don't get it" — 7 ways in

### Explain it with a compass
In grid country, use your compass to check the road rather than the other way round. Stand on a section road, sight along it, and read the bearing. If it comes out within a degree of a cardinal, the road and your compass have checked each other and you may use either; if it is three degrees off, one of them is wrong and you have learned something important before you needed it. Do this once in an area and you have either a free, continuous, drift-free direction for the rest of the day, or a warning — and both are worth the minute.

### Explain it with a map
Zoom out until you can see twenty miles at once, and look only at the pattern of roads. The grid shows instantly: roads a mile apart, meeting at right angles, with occasional jogs where the correction lines are. Then look for where the pattern ends. In Ohio the western boundary of the Virginia Military District is visible from space as a seam between order and tangle. Nothing on a map tells you more per second of looking, and you can do it before you leave the house.

### Use an everyday object
Look at the tiles in a bathroom and then at the floorboards in an old house. The tiles were set out from a line somebody chose, so the grout runs true to the room and you could set a spirit level by it. The boards were laid along the joists, which were laid along whatever the builders had, and they run how they run. Both are straight; only one of them was aimed. From inside the room, with the floor covered, you could not tell which you were standing on.

### Show me a diagram
Switch between the two sheets a few times and watch only the shape of the lines. On the grid, every line is one of two directions and the whole picture is built from those two. On the other, no two lines agree and the longest feature on the sheet is the stream the boundaries are following. Then watch the numbers above: the first readout moves from minutes of arc to tens of degrees. Nothing else about the ground has changed.

### Walk me through an example
You are on a section road in western Ohio in thick fog. You checked it this morning against a bearing you trusted and it came out within half a degree of due east, which is eighteen yards in a mile — three times better than the compass in your pocket — so you can walk it for an hour and hold a line you could not hold by hand. Now drive forty miles east into the Virginia Military District and try the same thing. The road you are on was laid along a boundary that ran from a sycamore to a creek; it averages twenty-two degrees off the nearest cardinal, and you do not know which cardinal. In the first place the road is an instrument you have checked. In the second it is a river.

### Break it into smaller steps
Four questions for any human mark. One: did somebody choose its direction, or did its direction come from the ground? Two: if somebody chose, what were they aiming at — a cardinal, a boundary, a crossing, another building? Three: is that choice documented anywhere, or am I supplying the reason myself? Four: how much would it cost me to be wrong about this? The third question is the one that separates a surveyed road from a bent tree, and it is the only one that can be answered from a library.

### Work backward from the answer
Work backwards from the surprise: the best compass in this entire course is a road, and it is better than the instrument by a factor of twenty-seven. How can a road be more accurate than a device built for the purpose? Because the accuracy, where it exists, was not produced where you are standing. It was produced once, by surveyors with a chain and a great deal of walking, and then stored in the landscape where it does not drift, does not need declination, and cannot be misread by a tired person at dusk. Where they got it right it is superb; where they did not, it is wrong in the same way for miles, which is why one check settles it. Everything in this course that worked well worked like that — a difficult measurement made once, carefully, and then carried.
