_Opening question._ How far north are you, to the nearest forty miles?

## SEE IT
*The Professor:* Change your latitude and watch one number follow another.

**On-screen simulation:** `star-sky` ({"mode":"latitude"})

**What it shows.** The horizon view again, with a latitude slider in place of the list of towns, and Polaris's own small circle round the true pole drawn at six times its real size — at true scale it would be invisible, which is itself the point. The readout gives Polaris's height and the chosen latitude side by side and names the difference.

**What the learner can change.** Latitude from 2° to 72° north, the month, and the hour of the night. Moving the latitude slider moves Polaris up and down degree for degree; moving the hour slider makes it wobble.

**The numbers behind it.** The pole's height above the horizon equals the observer's latitude exactly, by geometry. Polaris is 37.7 arcminutes (0.63°) from the pole in 2026, so its height swings 0.63° either side of the latitude across a night: at Columbus, 39.33° to 40.59° for a true 39.96°. That is 38 nautical miles — 43 statute — of position error from reading the star without noting the hour. Polaris was 44.2 arcminutes out in 2000 and will be nearest the pole, about 27.6 arcminutes, around 2100.

> _For the handbook:_ Chapter 7 should give the Polaris correction as a usable table rather than as a caution, because a reader who knows the error exists and cannot remove it will simply ignore it. The chapter must also say early and plainly that the method fails entirely south of the equator and badly near it, where the star is too low for refraction to be ignored — a method that covers a third of the Earth should say so in its first paragraph, not its last.

> **Field Guide — Chapter 7: Navigating by the Stars.** Measure the height of Polaris with your fist — knuckles are about ten degrees — and compare the answer with your latitude from a map. Agreement within a fist's width is a real measurement of where you are on the planet, taken with your hand, and it is the oldest position fix there is.

**What the learner should notice**
- Drag the latitude slider and Polaris rises and falls with it, degree for degree. At 40° north it sits about 40° up; at 25° north, about 25°. The sky is telling you where you are.
- "About" is doing work. Move through the night at a fixed latitude and the height changes by up to **1.26°** from end to end — because Polaris is not at the pole and circles it once a day.
- That swing is **0.63°** either side, which is **38 nautical miles** of latitude. The star gives you your position to within a county, free, and no better than that unless you do something about it.

## UNDERSTAND IT
*The Professor:* Three sentences. The first is one of the most useful facts in navigation.

**The three sentences**
1. **The pole's height above your horizon is your latitude.** Not approximately, not usually — exactly, by geometry. Stand at the north pole and the celestial pole is straight overhead, 90° up, and your latitude is 90°. Stand on the equator and it sits on your horizon at 0°, and your latitude is 0°. Everywhere between, the two numbers move together because they are the same angle measured from two ends.
2. **Polaris is a stand-in for the pole, and a slightly loose one.** It is 37.7 arcminutes away, so it rides a small circle around the true pole once a day — sometimes above it, sometimes below, sometimes to one side. Its height is your latitude plus or minus up to 0.63°, depending entirely on the hour and the date.
3. And **the error is in your position, not your direction.** A bearing that is 0.63° off costs you about twenty yards in a mile, which is nothing. A latitude that is 0.63° off puts you **38 nautical miles** from where you think you are, which off a coast at night is everything. The same small angle means two very different things depending on what you are using it for.

### A CLOCK WITH A BENT SECOND HAND
Imagine a clock whose second hand is mounted very slightly off centre, so it wobbles as it goes round. Over a minute it reads correctly on average, but at any instant it may be a second or two out — and which way depends on where in the circle it happens to be. You can live with that, or you can learn the pattern and correct for it. What you cannot do is read it once and call the answer exact. Polaris is the bent hand, and its circle takes a day.

The limitation the brief asks for, stated plainly: **this does not work in the southern hemisphere at all**, because Polaris is below the horizon from everywhere south of the equator. It also fails near the equator, where the star sits so low that haze and refraction near the horizon swamp a measurement you can barely take. The method covers perhaps a third of the Earth's surface well, and that is worth knowing before you rely on it.

### The formal names
| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Latitude** | How far north or south of the equator you are. The quantity this whole lesson produces, and the easier half of the position problem — longitude is Week 6 and took Europe two centuries. |
| **Pole height** | The angle from your horizon up to the celestial pole. Equal to your latitude, exactly, and the reason any of this works. |
| **Polaris correction** | The amount to add or subtract to turn an observed Polaris height into the true pole height. Published as a table against the hour and date; up to 0.63° in 2026. |
| **Nautical mile** | One minute of latitude — about 1.15 statute miles. Invented exactly so that an angle on the sky converts straight into a distance on the sea. |
| **Altitude error** | Any error in a measured height. One arcminute of it is one nautical mile of position, which is why sextants read to fractions of a minute. |
| **Dip** | At sea, the horizon sits slightly below true level because you are above the water — about a degree from a high deck. A real sight corrects for it; a fist measurement does not pretend to. |
| **Refraction** | The atmosphere lifting low objects. Near the horizon it is worth half a degree and badly behaved, which is why a star low in the sky is a poor one to measure. |
| **Sextant** | The instrument for measuring the angle between a star and the horizon accurately. Your fist is the same measurement with three orders of magnitude less precision. |

## CONNECT IT
*The Roommate:* Reading one quantity off another is something you do constantly.

**Where it shows up**
- Judging how late it is from how low the sun is.
- Telling how full a jar is by its weight.
- Knowing the oven is ready by the sound of the fan.
- Guessing how far away a storm is by counting after the lightning.
- Reading a petrol gauge, which measures a float and reports a distance.
- Telling someone's height from where they come up against a doorframe.

The lightning one is the closest relative: a count of seconds becomes a distance in miles because you know the rate that connects them. Polaris's height becomes your latitude the same way — and like the thunder count, it has a built-in error you can either ignore or learn.

### Across cultures — THE INDIAN OCEAN, 15TH CENTURY
**Evidence label: WELL DOCUMENTED**

Indian Ocean navigators measured the height of Polaris in **isba** — finger-widths — using a *kamal*: a small rectangle of wood on a knotted cord. You hold the cord in your teeth at a particular knot, which fixes the distance from your eye, and the card then subtends a known angle; you slide to the knot at which the card's bottom edge sits on the horizon and its top edge on the star. One isba is about **1°36′**, and the knots are stepped in isba and fractions of them.

What the instrument really does is remove the body from the measurement. A fist at arm's length varies from person to person; a card at a cord's length does not, and two navigators with the same kamal get the same number. Ports were described by their Polaris height in isba, so a navigator sailed north or south until the star read the right number and then ran east or west along it — latitude sailing, done with a piece of wood and a string.

> **What remains uncertain:** The precise value of an isba is not settled: sources in English give 1°36′, 1°37′ and "about one and a half degrees", and the unit was in any case defined by practice rather than by standard, so it likely varied between regions and between navigators. How far the kamal was used for routine position-finding rather than for recognising a known destination's latitude is also hard to judge from texts that assume their readers already know the craft.  
> **Sources:** Tibbetts, *Arab Navigation in the Indian Ocean before the Coming of the Portuguese* (1971); Ibn Mājid, *Kitāb al-Fawāʾid* (1490), translated in Tibbetts; Taylor, *The Haven-Finding Art* (1956, rev. 1971).

_Do not restate this claim in the handbook without its label, its uncertainty and its sources._

Explain to someone how a star's height above the horizon tells you how far north you are — and tell them honestly how wrong the answer will be, in miles, if they measure it carelessly.

Banned words: **altitude**, **equals**, **coincidence**

**A good explanation contains**
- It explains WHY the two angles are the same — by walking through the pole and the equator as the two extreme cases, or by the geometry.
- It says the star is not quite at the pole and gives the size of that gap.
- It converts the error into a distance on the ground, with a number.
- It says where on Earth the method does not work.

## CHALLENGE IT
*The Editor:* A prediction, and a question with a tidy answer.

**You measure Polaris at 41.2° above the horizon. Your chart says you are at 40.6° north. Which is most likely?**

- _Both are right: Polaris is near the top of its little circle round the pole._  
  The most likely answer by far. The gap is 0.6°, which is almost exactly the radius of Polaris's circle, so this is the textbook case of an uncorrected sight taken at the wrong hour. A navigator would consult a correction table — or note where the Dipper and Cassiopeia sit, which tells you where in the circle Polaris currently is.

- _The chart is out of date._  
  Latitude does not drift. Magnetic declination does, which is Lesson 7, and the pole star's position does over centuries, which is precession — but your latitude is where you are standing, and a chart from 1890 has it as right as one from last week.

- _You measured badly._  
  Possible, and with a fist it is likely — but notice that a measurement error and the Polaris circle produce exactly the same symptom here. The way to tell them apart is to take the sight again a few hours later: a measurement error will be random, while the circle will have moved in a predictable direction.

**Polaris gives you latitude. What stops the same trick from giving you longitude?**

Nothing in the sky's geometry is tied to a particular meridian. Latitude works because the Earth's axis gives the sky a built-in north–south reference that everybody shares: the pole is overhead at the pole and on the horizon at the equator, for everyone, always. There is no equivalent east–west landmark, because the Earth spins about that axis and carries every meridian past the same stars in turn.

So longitude needs something the sky does not supply: a **time** reference. If you know the local time from the sky and also know what time it is at some agreed meridian, the difference is your longitude at fifteen degrees an hour — which is Lesson 14's conversion, used backwards. That is why longitude waited for clocks and latitude did not, and it is the whole of Week 6.

## "I don't get it" — 7 ways in

### Explain it with a compass
A compass answers "which way", and this answers "how far north" — different questions, and this week has quietly handed you the harder one. Notice what they have in common though: both rely on the Earth having an axis. The compass works because the planet's field is roughly aligned with it; the pole height works because the sky's rotation is exactly aligned with it. Take the spin away and neither instrument has anything to say.

### Explain it with a map
Every horizontal line on your map is a line of latitude, and each one is a line along which the pole star sits at a particular height. Measuring its height picks out which line you are on — it does not tell you where along the line. That is exactly the "line of position" idea from Lesson 10, arriving again from a completely different direction: one measurement gives you a line, and you need a second, different measurement to get a point.

### Use an everyday object
Stand a protractor on a table with a drinking straw taped along its edge and a weighted thread hanging from the middle. Sight along the straw at Polaris and read where the thread crosses the scale: ninety minus that number is the star's height. That is a quadrant, which is the whole instrument, and a version of it was good enough to get Portuguese ships down the coast of Africa and back.

### Show me a diagram
In the simulation, Polaris sits on a small dashed circle drawn six times its real size — otherwise it would be invisible at this scale, which is itself worth noticing. The centre of that circle is the true pole. Move the night slider and Polaris travels round it. The readout shows its height and your latitude side by side, so you can watch one wobble around the other.

### Walk me through an example
You are at 39.96° north in Columbus. The true pole is therefore exactly 39.96° above your northern horizon, all night, every night, for ever. Polaris sits 0.63° from the pole, so at the best moment it reads 39.96 and at the worst it reads 39.33 or 40.59. Convert the worst case: 0.63° × 60 = 38 nautical miles, which is the distance from Columbus to Springfield. That is how far wrong you can be while taking a perfectly careful sight.

### Break it into smaller steps
Two claims, and only the first is hard. One: the celestial pole's height above your horizon equals your latitude. Test it at the two ends — at the north pole it is overhead and you are at 90°, at the equator it is on the horizon and you are at 0° — and if it holds at both ends and changes smoothly, it holds between. Two: Polaris is nearly at the celestial pole, so its height is nearly the same number. Everything else in this lesson is about the word "nearly".

### Work backward from the answer
Start from the claim that 0.63° of sky equals 38 nautical miles of sea. Why would an angle convert to a distance at all? Because a degree of latitude is a fixed arc on the Earth's surface — about 60 nautical miles, by the definition of the nautical mile. So any error in the angle you measure is an error in your position, at that exchange rate. Work it further back and you reach the reason the nautical mile exists: somebody wanted exactly this conversion to need no arithmetic.
