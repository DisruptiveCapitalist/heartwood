_Opening question._ A compass needle has nothing to push against. No string, no motor, nobody touching it. Spin it and it swings around, settles — and settles the same way every time. What is holding it there?

## SEE IT
*The Professor:* Start with a magnet on a table. Move the little compass around it and watch what the needle actually does.

**On-screen simulation:** `magnet-field`

**What it shows.** Two tabs, 760 × 470. **A magnet on the table** draws a bar magnet 128 px long with its field as nine traced lines, and a small compass — a 26 px rose with a red north end — sitting at one of four spots around it, turned to whatever the field is doing there. **The Earth itself** draws the globe with a bar magnet inside it, tilted from the dashed spin axis, eleven field lines arcing from one end round to the other, and a compass lying flat on the ground at a chosen magnetic latitude, tipping with the field. Readouts give the needle's angle and the relative strength on the first tab, and the dip angle and the usable fraction of the pull on the second.

**What the learner can change.** First tab: four places to put the compass (straight above, off to the side, up at the corner, underneath), a toggle for the field lines, and a button that turns the magnet round so every needle flips. Second tab: four places to stand — the magnetic equator, Ohio, Anchorage, the pole — and the same field-lines toggle.

**The numbers behind it.** The bar magnet is two point poles 128 px apart; the Earth is a single point dipole at the centre, with its axis tilted 9.21° from the axis of rotation (NOAA NCEI, WMM2025 at 2025.0). Everything drawn and every number shown comes from the same field function, so the needle cannot disagree with the lines around it. The dipole gives the textbook relation exactly, checked at six latitudes: tan(dip) = 2 × tan(magnetic latitude) — 0° and 100% of the pull usable at the magnetic equator, 66.5° and 40% at magnetic latitude 49°, 75.1° and 26% at 62°, 89.0° and 2% at 88°. Field strength is quoted in the lesson as a range, 25 to 65 µT, not as a local figure. The geomagnetic pole is 80.85° N, 72.76° W and the dip pole 85.76° N, 139.30° E — about 894 miles apart.

> _For the handbook:_ The one thing a diagram must get right is that the needle lies **along** a field line and does not point at the magnet. Drawing the compass at the side position, where the line runs nearly parallel to the magnet rather than towards it, makes that unmistakable. For the Earth, draw the inside magnet with its **south** pole under the Arctic and label it so; and draw the field entering the ground steeply at mid-northern latitudes rather than running level, because the steepness is what the dip readout is about. This model is good for shape and poor for the local angle — the Field Guide's Chapter 9 figures should come from the published model, as Lesson 7's do.

> **Field Guide — Chapter 8: Understanding the Magnetic Compass.** Magnetising a sewing needle on a fridge magnet and floating it on cork in a bowl of water, well clear of steel, then checking it against a real compass — the simulation's little compass, built by hand.

**What the learner should notice**
- The needle never points **at** the magnet. It lies down along whichever line runs through the spot it happens to be sitting on.
- Turn the magnet round and every needle near it turns too. The needle isn't choosing anything — it is being told, by the place it is in.
- Switch to the Earth and the same picture appears, enormously larger. At Ohio's magnetic latitude the field dives **67° below level**, so only **40%** of the pull is the sideways part a flat compass can use.

## UNDERSTAND IT
*The Professor:* Three sentences. The third one is the one that makes a compass make sense.

**The three sentences**
1. A **magnet** has two ends that behave oppositely, and you cannot have one without the other — snap one in half and you get two whole magnets, each with both ends. Unlike ends pull together; like ends shove apart.
2. A magnet does not reach out and grab. It changes the space around it, so that every point nearby acquires a **direction**, and anything magnetic put there feels a twist until it lines up with that direction. Space with a direction at every point is a **field**, and the lines drawn in SEE IT are only a way of making one visible.
3. The Earth has one, generated by moving molten iron deep inside it. To a first approximation it is a single bar magnet at the centre, tilted about **9.2°** from the axis the Earth spins on — and the end of that magnet lying under the Arctic is its **south** pole. Which is precisely why the north end of your needle is drawn towards it.

### A FIELD FULL OF WEATHERVANES
Picture a meadow with a thousand weathervanes in it. The wind does not pick one out and push it; it is simply present everywhere, and every vane swings until it agrees with the air where it stands. You can walk through and read the wind off the vanes without feeling anything yourself. A compass needle is a weathervane, and magnetism is the wind — except this wind pours out of one end of a magnet, arcs over, and sinks back into the other, and it never stops blowing.

Two things worth carrying away about the Earth's field. First, it is **weak** — somewhere between about 25 and 65 microtesla at the surface, depending where you stand. A cheap refrigerator magnet is roughly a hundred times stronger than that close up, which is why a magnet loose in your pocket will wreck a compass reading. Second, it is **not level**. It comes out of the ground near Antarctica, arcs over the planet, and dives back in near the Arctic, so by the time it reaches the northern United States it is already running steeply downward. Compasses sold for the northern hemisphere have the needle quietly weighted on its south end to stop it grounding against the glass — carry that same compass to Australia and it will drag.

### The formal names
| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Magnetic pole** | One of the two ends of a magnet. They always come in pairs; nobody has ever found one on its own. |
| **Magnetic field** | The direction, at every point in space, that a freely turning needle would settle along. Drawn as lines — but the lines are a picture, not a thing you could trip over. |
| **Field line** | A line drawn so that its direction at every point is the field's direction there. Lines close together mean a strong field. |
| **Lodestone** | Naturally magnetised rock — the mineral magnetite. The first compasses in the world were made from it. |
| **Geographic north pole** | Where the spin axis comes out of the ground. True north. It does not move. |
| **Geomagnetic pole** | Where the axis of the Earth's "bar magnet" comes out: 80.85° N, 72.76° W in 2025. The tilt from the spin axis is 9.21°. |
| **Magnetic pole (dip pole)** | Where the field actually points straight down: 85.76° N, 139.30° E in 2025 — about 900 miles from the geomagnetic pole, and wandering. Two different things are called "magnetic north," and this is the one in the newspapers. |
| **Inclination, or dip** | How far below level the field points. 0° at the magnetic equator, 90° at a dip pole. |
| **Microtesla (µT)** | The unit field strength is measured in. The Earth manages 25 to 65 of them. |
| **Declination** | How far the needle sits from true north, where you are standing. All of tomorrow's lesson. |

## CONNECT IT

**Where it shows up**
- A fridge magnet that refuses to sit the way you want it and flips itself over.
- Two magnets that snap together one way round and shove each other apart the other.
- A phone compass asking you to wave the phone in a figure-eight before it will work.
- Headphones or a small speaker sticking to the fridge — there is a magnet inside.
- A hard drive or a hotel key card warning you to keep it away from magnets.
- A compass going peculiar near a car, a steel gate, or a camera tripod.

Every one of those is the same thing twice over: a direction that was already present in the space, and something small enough and free enough to turn until it agrees.

### The Builder — try it yourself
Two minutes tonight, and you will have built the oldest instrument in this course. Take a sewing needle and stroke it along one end of a fridge magnet twenty times — always the same direction, lifting it clear between strokes. Lay the needle on a slice of cork or a plastic bottle cap, and float it in a bowl of water on a wooden or plastic table, well away from anything steel. It will turn, and settle, and settle the same way every time you spin it. Check it against a real compass or a phone. You have just made the thing described in the card below, about a thousand years after somebody wrote the instructions down.

### Across cultures — CHINA, HAN TO SONG
**Evidence label: WELL DOCUMENTED**

Chinese writers knew about lodestone — naturally magnetised rock — a long way back. Around 80 CE, Wang Chong's *Lunheng* mentions a "south-pointing" instrument. By the eleventh century the texts are unambiguous: the *Wujing Zongyao*, a military compendium of about 1044, gives working instructions for a thin iron fish, heated and then cooled while held north-to-south, and floated in a bowl of water to show direction.

A few decades later, in the *Mengxi Bitan* — Dream Pool Essays, about 1088 — the scholar Shen Kuo described four ways of mounting a magnetised needle, compared them, and settled on hanging it from a single strand of silk as the steadiest. He also wrote down something nobody is known to have recorded before: the needle does not point true south, but a little to the east of it. That is the gap tomorrow's whole lesson is about, noticed in China roughly four hundred years before it was written down in Europe.

> **What remains uncertain:** The famous "south-pointing spoon" — a lodestone ladle resting on a bronze plate, pictured in many books as a Han-dynasty compass — is a twentieth-century reconstruction by the historian Wang Zhenduo, built from brief textual descriptions. No such object has ever been excavated, and whether the Han instrument worked that way at all, or was used for divination rather than for finding direction, is still argued over. Wang Chong's phrase is short enough to be read several ways.  
> **Sources:** Needham, *Science and Civilisation in China*, vol. 4 part 1 (1962); Shen Kuo, *Mengxi Bitan*, translated in Needham; Wang Zhenduo, "Discovery and Application of Magnetic Phenomena in China," *Chinese Journal of Archaeology* (1948).

_Do not restate this claim in the handbook without its label, its uncertainty and its sources._

## FEYNMAN IT
Explain to a Scout why a compass needle points north — **without using the words "attract," "pull," or "grab."** Nothing far away is tugging on it.

Banned words: **attract**, **pull**, **grab**

**A good explanation contains**
- The Earth behaves like one enormous magnet, with its ends up near the poles.
- That gives every spot on the surface a direction — not a destination.
- A needle free to turn settles along the direction at the spot where it sits.
- So it is not reaching for the Pole. It is agreeing with the ground underneath it.

## CHALLENGE IT
*The Editor:* One about magnets, and one that takes the cartoon apart.

**Snap a bar magnet cleanly in half, straight across the middle. What are you holding?**

- _Two magnets, each with both ends._  
  That's it, and it keeps going. Halve them again and you have four, each with a north end and a south end. Grind the whole thing to powder and every speck is still a tiny complete magnet. Nobody has ever isolated a single magnetic end — people have looked hard, with large machines, and come back empty-handed. This is genuinely different from electricity, where you can have a lone positive charge quite easily.

- _One piece that is all north, and one that is all south._  
  It is the obvious guess, and it is the one physics refuses to give you. Break the magnet and each half immediately has both ends — the fresh faces become a new south and a new north. It happens at the cut, instantly, every time. Whatever a magnet is made of, the two-endedness goes all the way down: the smallest piece you can imagine still has both.

- _Two pieces, neither of them magnetic any more._  
  Breaking a magnet does weaken it a little — the pieces are shorter, so their ends are closer together and the field outside is less impressive. But magnetic they remain. Each half will pick up a paperclip, snap onto the other half one way round, and shove it away the other. The magnetism is a property of the material all through, not something stored in the middle that can leak out.

**Suppose you carried your compass all the way to the magnetic pole — the real one, where the field points straight down. What would the needle do when you arrived?**

It would try to point **straight down**, and a flat compass cannot do that. The needle would simply sit there, dragging on the glass, telling you nothing. This is the thing the whole lesson has been circling: a needle is not aiming at a place, it is lying down along the field — and at the dip pole the field goes vertically into the ground.

You can watch it coming. Go back to SEE IT, press **The Earth itself**, and walk north. At the magnetic equator the field is level and every bit of it is usable. At Ohio's magnetic latitude it already dives 67° and only 40% of the pull is sideways. In Anchorage, 75° and 26%. At the pole, 90° and nothing at all. A compass is an instrument that works beautifully across most of the world and stops working precisely where the children's-book picture says it should work best.

## "I don't get it" — 7 ways in

### Explain it with a compass
Put a compass flat on a table and watch the needle settle. Now turn the whole compass slowly through a full circle. The housing turns, the numbers turn, the baseplate turns — and the needle does not. It stays exactly where it was, while everything else rotates around it. That is the whole idea in one move: the needle is not attached to the instrument, it is attached to the room.

### Explain it with a map
Think of a weather map covered in little wind arrows, one in every square. Nobody imagines the arrows are being tugged towards a particular town — they are just saying what the air is doing in that square. A magnetic field map looks exactly the same, and means exactly the same kind of thing: here is what a free needle would do, at this spot. Your compass is reading one square of that map, the one you are standing in.

### Use an everyday object
Get two fridge magnets and slide one slowly towards the other across the table. At some distance the second one suddenly twitches and spins to face the first. Now turn one over and try again — this time it shoves backwards. Nothing touched it either time. The space between them was already different because of the first magnet, and the second one simply reported what that space was doing.

### Show me a diagram
Go back to SEE IT, stay on the first tab, and press **Put the compass: off to the side**. Look at where the needle is pointing. It is not pointing at the magnet — it is pointing along the curved line that passes under it, which at that spot runs nearly parallel to the magnet rather than towards it. Then press **Underneath** and watch the needle flip completely. Same magnet, same room, opposite answer, because the line through that spot runs the other way.

### Walk me through an example
Here is Shen Kuo's own experiment, in modern form. Magnetise a needle by stroking it on a magnet. Hang it from a single thread so that it is free to turn in any direction at all. Let it stop. Now carry it to the other side of the room and let it stop again — same direction. Take it outdoors, same direction. The one thing it is agreeing with is present in every one of those places, and it is present whether or not a needle is there to report it.

### Break it into smaller steps
Three small questions. One: does the needle move when you move the magnet? Yes. Two: does anything physical connect them — string, air current, contact? No. Three: so what travels between them? Nothing travels. The magnet changes what the space itself is like, everywhere at once, and the needle reports the space it is in. "Field" is just the name for space that has been changed in this way.

### Work backward from the answer
Start from a fact that makes no sense on the cartoon version. A compass is useless at the magnetic pole — the needle just dips and sticks. If the needle were being drawn towards a place, standing on that place would be the one situation where it worked perfectly. It is the opposite. Work backward from that and you are forced to the right picture: the needle lies along the field, and over the pole the field runs straight down.
