_Opening question._ A compass knows direction and nothing else. A map knows everything except where you are and which way you are facing. Put them in the same two hands and each one supplies exactly what the other is missing.

## SEE IT
*The Professor:* You are at the trail junction and you can see the fire tower. Take the reading, do the arithmetic, and swing the line round until it lands on it.

**On-screen simulation:** `compass` ({"mode":"map"})

**What it shows.** A map sheet 760 × 560 with a grid, north up, three named landmarks on it, and a marker reading "you are here" at the trail junction. A dashed line swings out from that marker at whatever angle the learner sets; when it lands on the chosen landmark the line and the landmark's ring turn green. A corner shows true north as a black arrow, and optionally magnetic north as a red one leaning 7.2° from it. Three readouts give the compass reading, the true bearing it converts to, and how far off the drawn line currently is.

**What the learner can change.** Three landmark buttons choose what you sighted on, a slider swings the drawn line, and a toggle shows or hides magnetic north beside true north on the sheet.

**The numbers behind it.** Bearings computed from the sheet, with the declination −7.22° (Columbus, Ohio; NOAA NCEI WMM2025 at 1 January 2026): Beaver Pond is 014.7° true and reads 021.9° magnetic; the fire tower 052.9° and 060.2°; Hollow Rock 084.0° and 091.2°. True = magnetic + declination, so 60.2 + (−7.2) = 53.0, and the line drawn at 053 true passes through the tower. Drawn at the magnetic number instead it misses by 7.2°, which over two miles of walking is 446 yards. The line counts as on the landmark within 2°.

> _For the handbook:_ Chapter 11's diagram should show the same line drawn twice from one known point — once at the magnetic number and once at the true one — with the gap between them labelled in yards rather than degrees, because degrees look negligible and yards do not. The line must run off the edge of the sheet rather than stopping at the landmark: a single bearing gives a line, not a point, and the picture should not quietly suggest otherwise. Finding your own position from crossed bearings is resection, and belongs with Chapter 12.

> **Field Guide — Chapter 11: Using a Compass with a Map.** The week's challenge: a short four-leg course in a park you know, planned on paper with bearings and distances, converted to magnetic with your own declination, and walked back to the starting point with a road always in sight.

**What the learner should notice**
- The compass says **060° magnetic** to the fire tower. Draw 060 on the map and the line misses it — the map is drawn to true north and the compass is not.
- Add the declination: 60.2 + (−7.2) = **053° true**. Draw that and the line goes straight through the tower.
- Seven degrees looks like nothing on a dial. Across this sheet's two miles it is **446 yards** — the difference between finding the tower and walking past it.

## UNDERSTAND IT
*The Professor:* Three sentences, and the week is yours.

**The three sentences**
1. **Orienting the map** is the first thing to do and the thing most often skipped: turn the sheet on the ground until its north points at north, and from then on everything on the paper lies in the direction of the thing it stands for. The ridge drawn to the left of your position is now actually on your left. You can do it with the compass, or with two landmarks you can see and identify.
2. **Transferring a bearing from ground to map**: take the reading with the compass, convert it to true, then lay the baseplate's edge through your known position and turn the whole compass until the printed line runs at that angle to the map's north. Draw along the edge. Everything along that line is in the direction you were looking.
3. What that line is good for depends on what you already know. From a position you know, a bearing to something you can see tells you **where that thing is** — you have plotted an object. Not knowing where you are is a different and harder problem, and it needs two or three bearings crossing; that is **resection**, and it is Week 5.

### TURNING THE PAGE ROUND
Everybody has done this with a mall directory or a station map: you stand in front of it, see that you want the place marked bottom-left, set off left — and it is the wrong way, because the board was drawn facing the other direction. So you turn your head sideways, or you walk round the board, or you give up and mentally rotate the whole thing and get it wrong anyway. Orienting a map is simply refusing to do the mental rotation. Turn the paper instead of your brain, and the map stops being a diagram you have to translate and becomes a picture of what is actually in front of you.

There are two ways to orient a map and they fail in different circumstances, so learn both. **With the compass**: set your declination at the index line — your actual local figure — then put the baseplate's long edge along a north-south grid line on the map, and turn the map and compass together, as one object, until the needle drops into the orienting arrow. The sheet is now square with the world. **By eye**: find two things you can both see and identify on the map — a water tower, a road junction, a lake's end — and turn the sheet until both of them line up with where they really are. The eye method needs visibility and two known things; the compass method needs neither, but it needs you to have remembered the declination. On a clear day the two should agree, and checking one against the other costs ten seconds and has saved a great many people a long walk.

### The formal names
| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Orienting the map** | Turning the sheet until its north lines up with north on the ground. Sometimes called setting the map. |
| **Grid lines** | The printed north-south and east-west lines you lay the compass along. They point to grid north, which is near enough true north for walking. |
| **Plotting an object** | Drawing a bearing from a position you know, so that something you can see must lie along it. |
| **Resection** | The reverse: bearings to two or three known landmarks, crossed on the map, to find where *you* are. Week 5. |
| **Line of position** | Any line you have good reason to believe you are on — a bearing, a road, a ridge crest, a shoreline. Lesson 1's shapes, with better instruments. |
| **Handrail** | A long feature running roughly your way — a stream, a wall, a track — that you follow instead of walking a bearing. Easier and far harder to lose. |
| **Catching feature** | Something unmissable beyond your target that tells you you have gone too far. A road, a river, a change of slope. |
| **Attack point** | An obvious feature near a hard-to-find target. Navigate loosely to the attack point, then take a careful short bearing from there. |

## CONNECT IT
*The Roommate:* Lining a drawing up with the world. Which of these have you done?

**Where it shows up**
- Turning a mall directory map around in your head and setting off the wrong way.
- Rotating a phone map with two fingers so the street ahead is at the top.
- Matching a furniture diagram to the actual pile of parts before starting.
- Holding a seating chart the right way round so section numbers match the stadium.
- Turning a recipe's pan diagram until it matches the pan on the stove.
- Standing at a trailhead sign working out which of the paths in front of you is which.

Each of those is the same small act of humility: instead of translating the picture in your head, you turn the picture until it agrees with the world. It is the cheapest navigation technique there is, and the one people skip.

### The Builder — try it yourself
This is the week's field exercise, and the brief asks for it to be short and safe. In a park or open ground you know well, set a course of four legs that brings you back to where you started — a rough square is ideal. Before you go, draw it on a sketch of the area and work out each leg's bearing and rough distance. Convert every one to magnetic using your own looked-up declination, and write them in a list. Then walk it: set each bearing, pick a landmark, count your paces, and tick the leg off. Keep a road or a building you recognise in sight the whole way; take water, and tell somebody where you are going. The measure of success is finishing within sight of your starting point — and if you do not, the interesting question is which leg went wrong and how you would know.

### Across cultures — GREAT BRITAIN, 1935 ONWARD
**Evidence label: WELL DOCUMENTED**

The Ordnance Survey began resurveying the whole of Great Britain in 1935, under its Director General Malcolm MacLeod — the Retriangulation, with triangulation fieldwork starting in April 1936 and the main work not finished until 1962. The concrete trig pillars still standing on British hilltops are its monuments. Out of it came the OSGB36 datum and the National Grid: a single set of squares laid over the whole country, so that any point could be named by two numbers read off the same sheet.

Laying a flat grid over a curved country is Lesson 4's bargain again, and it has a consequence that lands in this lesson. The grid is built on a Transverse Mercator projection with its origin at 49° N, 2° W, and its vertical lines run exactly true north **only along that central meridian**. Everywhere else they lean slightly, which is why a British map's margin shows three norths rather than two: true, magnetic, and grid. The one you lay your compass along is the third.

> **What remains uncertain:** Sources differ over whether the OSGB36 datum is best described as introduced during or after the 1936–62 retriangulation, and the figures given for how far grid north departs from true north vary with where in the country you are standing and which document you read — so this lesson does not quote one. For your own map, read the margin rather than any general rule.  
> **Sources:** Seymour (ed.), *A History of the Ordnance Survey* (1980); Hewitt, *Map of a Nation: A Biography of the Ordnance Survey* (2010); Ordnance Survey, *A Guide to Coordinate Systems in Great Britain*.

_Do not restate this claim in the handbook without its label, its uncertainty and its sources._

## FEYNMAN IT
Plan a short, safe course of three or four legs in a place you know, and write the instructions out so that somebody else could walk it with a map and compass and nothing else. Give each leg a bearing and a distance, say which north each bearing is, and name what they will see. Write it **without the words "GPS," "app," or "just head towards."**

Banned words: **gps**, **app**, **just head**

**A good explanation contains**
- Every leg has a bearing in three digits, and says whether it is magnetic or true.
- Every leg has a distance — in paces, yards or minutes — not just a direction.
- Every leg names something they will reach, so they know the leg worked.
- A stranger could walk the whole thing with a map and a compass and nothing else.

## CHALLENGE IT
*The Editor:* One about the arithmetic, and one about what this week could not give you.

**You are in Columbus (declination 7.2° west), standing somewhere you know. You take a bearing to a distant silo: **060 magnetic**. What do you draw on the map, and what does the line tell you?**

- _053 true from where you are standing — and the silo is somewhere along it._  
  Both halves right. The arithmetic: true = magnetic + declination, so 60 + (−7.2) = 52.8, call it 053. And the honest reading of what you have got: a line, not a point. The silo is on that line, but the line runs off the edge of the sheet and the bearing says nothing at all about how far along it the silo sits. Lesson 1 again — one good clue gives you a shape. Pair it with a second bearing from somewhere else and you would have the silo exactly.

- _060 from where you are standing — the compass said 060._  
  The compass did say 060, and that is a true statement about the compass. But the sheet in your other hand is drawn to true north, and the two norths are 7.2° apart in Columbus, so 060 on the paper is not the direction you were looking. The line would miss the silo by 7.2°, which over two miles of walking is 446 yards. This is the brief's warning in its most practical form: a compass bearing is not automatically a true-north bearing.

- _053 true — and where it crosses the silo on the map is where you are standing._  
  The arithmetic is right and the conclusion is the wrong way round, which is the confusion the brief specifically asks to be cleared up. You already knew where you were standing; that is what let you draw the line in the first place. What the line gives you is the silo's direction. Finding your *own* position from bearings to things you can see is a different operation — you need two or three of them, crossing — and it is called resection. Week 5.

**After five days you can take a bearing, walk it, and put it on a map. There is still one question you cannot answer with a compass. Which one — and does that bother you?**

Where you are. A compass is a direction instrument and nothing else; it has no idea of position, and five days of practice has not changed that. Everything this week has needed a known starting point — the trail junction, the car park gate, your own front door. Take that away and the compass goes quiet. This is Lesson 1's distinction coming back for the third time, and it is worth noticing that the course keeps returning to it rather than getting past it.

It should not bother you, because it is how the rest of the course is laid out. Week 3 and Week 4 go looking for position in the sky — the Sun's shortest shadow, and the height of Polaris above the horizon, both of which do tell you something a compass cannot. Week 5 crosses bearings to find a fix on paper. The compass turns out to be one instrument among several, each answering a different question, and the skill at the end is knowing which one you have just asked.

## "I don't get it" — 7 ways in

### Explain it with a compass
Here is the move in one go. Set your declination at the index line — not a bearing, your actual local figure. Lay the compass's long edge along any north-south grid line on the map, with the direction-of-travel arrow pointing up the sheet. Now pick up map and compass together, as a single object, and turn the pair until the needle drops into the kennel. Put it down. The map is now square with the world, and it will stay that way as long as you do not spin the sheet.

### Explain it with a map
Spread a map on a table and ignore the compass entirely. Find two things on it you can also see out of the window — a church, a hilltop, a tall building. Now turn the sheet until both of them point the right way at once. One alone is not enough; the sheet could still be flipped. Two of them, and there is only one way the paper can lie. That is orienting a map by eye, and it needs no instrument at all — only two things you can name.

### Use an everyday object
Think about assembling flat-pack furniture. The first thing anyone sensible does is turn the instruction sheet until the picture matches the heap of parts on the floor — same way up, same way round. Nobody tries to build the thing while mentally mirroring the diagram, because everybody has tried that once and ended up with a drawer that opens into the wall. A map is an instruction sheet for a landscape, and orienting it is turning the page the right way round before you start.

### Show me a diagram
Go back to SEE IT and press **Show both norths**. Two arrows appear in the corner, 7.2° apart — the black one the map is drawn to, the red one the compass answers to. Now swing the line round to 060, the number your compass gave you, and look at where it goes: past the tower, to the left. Swing it to 053 and it lands. The gap between those two positions of the same line, over two miles of paper, is the 446 yards.

### Walk me through an example
Work Hollow Rock through from scratch. You are at the trail junction and you can see the rock to the east. Compass up, aim at it, box the needle: **091 M**. Arithmetic: 91.2 plus the declination of −7.2 gives **084 T**. Now the map. Lay the baseplate's edge on the trail junction, swing the compass until the line reads 084 against the sheet's north, and draw. The line runs out east and passes through Hollow Rock. You have not learned where you are — you knew that — but you have proved the rock on the paper is the rock in front of you, which on an unfamiliar sheet is worth a great deal.

### Break it into smaller steps
Four small questions. One: does the map know which way it is lying on the ground? No — you have to tell it. Two: does the compass know what any of the things around you are called? No — the map does. Three: do they measure angles from the same north? No, and that gap is the week's arithmetic. Four: so what does each one need from the other? The map needs a direction; the compass needs names and distances. That is the whole partnership.

### Work backward from the answer
Start from a line drawn on a map and work backward to what somebody must have had. The line starts at a particular point, so they knew where they were. It runs at a particular angle to the sheet's north, so they had an instrument that finds north and they remembered to correct it. It stops nowhere in particular, because a bearing has no length. Three facts about the person, read off one pencil line — and the missing fourth, how far along it the thing sits, is exactly what the next three weeks go looking for.
